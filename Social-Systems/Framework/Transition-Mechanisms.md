# Purpose

This document defines the transition mechanisms responsible for transforming one social state into another.

The purpose is to distinguish **what changes the system** from **what constitutes the system**.

State variables describe the condition of the system.

Transition mechanisms describe the processes through which that condition evolves.

The Law of Motion will later describe how these mechanisms jointly transform the state.

---

# 1. Definition

A transition mechanism is a process that changes one or more state variables between two points in time.

Transition mechanisms are dynamic.

They are not themselves state variables.

---

# 2. Classification

The framework organizes transition mechanisms into five categories.

## I. Primitive Mechanisms

These operate continuously and arise directly from the four foundational axioms.

### 1. Heterogeneity

Differences among individuals and organizations generate different responses to identical environments.

Expected effect:

* divergence of trajectories;
* specialization;
* variation in opportunity utilization.

---

### 2. Institutional Translation

Institutions transform heterogeneous characteristics into rewards, responsibilities, opportunities, and constraints.

Expected effect:

* redistribution of incentives;
* differential access;
* institutional filtering.

---

### 3. Contingency

Historical events, timing, shocks, technological breakthroughs, and chance alter system trajectories.

Expected effect:

* path alteration;
* regime transition;
* nonlinear outcomes.

---

# II. Behavioral Mechanisms

These govern decentralized decision-making.

### 4. Decision Rules

Individuals and organizations respond to incentives, expectations, information, and constraints.

---

### 5. Role Activation

Individuals occupy multiple institutional and social roles.

Different contexts activate different roles.

Examples include:

* citizen;
* taxpayer;
* parent;
* employee;
* employer;
* investor.

---

### 6. Preference Formation

Activated roles interact with beliefs, information, identity, values, and expectations to produce context-dependent preferences.

---

### 7. Preference Aggregation

Individual preferences are aggregated through institutional mechanisms.

Examples include:

* elections;
* bargaining;
* markets;
* organizational governance;
* judicial processes.

---

# III. Evolutionary Mechanisms

These determine how short-run differences become long-run trajectories.

### 8. Cumulative Advantage

Initial advantages generate further advantages.

---

### 9. Path Dependence

Historical conditions constrain future possibilities.

---

### 10. Network Amplification

Interactions among individuals and organizations reinforce emerging patterns.

---

### 11. Survivorship and Selection

Successful strategies, organizations, and institutions become disproportionately represented over time.

---

# IV. Institutional Mechanisms

Institutions are active participants rather than static constraints.

### 12. Adaptive Intervention

Public and private interventions modify incentives, institutions, and allocation processes.

Interventions themselves alter future behavior.

---

### 13. Institutional Learning

Institutions accumulate experience through implementation, experimentation, and observation.

---

### 14. Institutional Self-Correction

Some institutions contain endogenous mechanisms for adjustment.

Examples include:

* elections;
* constitutional reform;
* judicial review;
* administrative reform;
* organizational learning.

Self-correction is typically delayed, contested, and incomplete.

---

### 15. Adaptive Capacity

Institutional quality includes the ability to recognize, process, and respond to changing conditions.

Adaptive capacity influences the effectiveness of institutional self-correction.

---

# V. Feedback Mechanisms

Feedback transforms current outcomes into future conditions.

### 16. Market Feedback

Prices, profits, wages, and resource allocation respond continuously.

---

### 17. Organizational Feedback

Organizations modify structure, incentives, and capabilities.

---

### 18. Political Feedback

Observed outcomes influence beliefs, coalitions, institutional reform, and governance.

---

### 19. Cultural Feedback

Norms, values, and social expectations evolve gradually through repeated interaction.

---

# 3. Interaction Among Mechanisms

Transition mechanisms rarely operate independently.

Typical interactions include:

* institutions influence decision rules;
* decision rules generate allocation;
* allocation modifies political preferences;
* political feedback alters institutions;
* institutions reshape future decision rules.

The framework therefore emphasizes interacting mechanisms rather than isolated causal effects.

---

# 4. Multiple Time Scales

Mechanisms operate at different speeds.

## Fast

* market responses;
* household decisions;
* firm behavior.

---

## Medium

* organizational adaptation;
* education;
* technology adoption;
* demographic adjustment.

---

## Slow

* legal evolution;
* political institutions;
* constitutions;
* cultural norms.

The interaction of different time scales may produce:

* delay;
* overshooting;
* oscillation;
* stability;
* instability;
* regime transition.

---

# 5. Criteria for Inclusion

A process should be classified as a transition mechanism only if it:

1. changes one or more state variables;
2. is conceptually distinct from the state itself;
3. contributes explanatory power;
4. can operate repeatedly over time;
5. can be represented qualitatively or quantitatively.

---

# 6. Open Research Questions

Several questions remain unresolved.

* Which mechanisms are universal?
* Which are application-specific?
* Which mechanisms dominate under different institutional regimes?
* Which mechanisms are nonlinear?
* Which mechanisms generate tipping points?
* How should interacting mechanisms be represented mathematically?

# Working Principle

Transition mechanisms should be viewed as reusable building blocks.

Different applications—social inequality, political polarization, institutional resilience, technological diffusion, or economic development—may involve different combinations and strengths of these mechanisms, while sharing the same underlying theoretical architecture.

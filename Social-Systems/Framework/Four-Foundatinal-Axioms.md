# Purpose

This document defines the four foundational axioms of the Dynamic Social Systems framework.

The axioms identify the minimum assumptions required for the existence of social evolution.

They are not empirical hypotheses to be tested individually. Rather, they provide the conceptual foundation from which the remainder of the framework is derived.

All subsequent components—including the state space, transition mechanisms, law of motion, transition operator, and empirical implementation—must remain consistent with these axioms.

---

# What Is an Axiom?

Within this framework, an axiom is a primitive assumption accepted as the starting point for theoretical development.

Axioms should satisfy four criteria.

They should be:

* conceptually fundamental;
* logically independent;
* broadly applicable across social systems;
* stable under changes in empirical application.

Mechanisms, institutions, policies, and historical events are **not** axioms.

They emerge from the interaction of the axioms.

---

# Axiom I — Heterogeneity

No social system consists of identical actors.

Individuals and organizations differ in:

* capabilities;
* preferences;
* information;
* resources;
* identities;
* experiences;
* opportunities;
* constraints.

These differences are persistent, although they evolve over time.

Without heterogeneity there would be no meaningful allocation problem and no endogenous source of differing social outcomes.

Heterogeneity is therefore the fundamental source of variation within the system.

---

# Axiom II — Institutional Translation

Differences among actors do not determine outcomes directly.

Institutions determine how heterogeneous characteristics are translated into opportunities, responsibilities, constraints, rewards, and social outcomes.

Institutions include formal and informal arrangements such as:

* legal systems;
* property rights;
* markets;
* governments;
* organizations;
* norms;
* conventions.

Institutional Translation therefore acts as the transformation layer connecting individual characteristics with observable outcomes.

---

# Axiom III — Contingency

Social evolution is continually influenced by historical contingency.

Contingencies include:

* technological breakthroughs;
* natural disasters;
* demographic shifts;
* geopolitical events;
* timing;
* chance.

Contingency does not eliminate systematic evolution.

Rather, it continually perturbs and redirects evolutionary trajectories.

Consequently, social evolution should be understood as adaptive rather than deterministic.

---

# Axiom IV — Recursive Feedback

Current outcomes influence future conditions.

Observed outcomes alter:

* institutions;
* capabilities;
* incentives;
* beliefs;
* organizations;
* opportunities;
* political coalitions.

Future evolution therefore depends upon previous evolution.

Social systems are recursive rather than memoryless.

This recursive structure transforms isolated historical events into long-run developmental trajectories.

---

# Collective Interpretation

Taken together, the four axioms describe a distributed adaptive system.

Heterogeneous actors interact through institutional structures under changing historical conditions.

Their interactions generate outcomes that subsequently modify the conditions governing future interactions.

The framework therefore views social evolution as an emergent process generated through continuous recursive adaptation rather than centralized control.

---

# What the Axioms Do Not Assume

The framework deliberately avoids assuming:

* perfectly rational actors;
* equilibrium;
* optimization;
* efficient markets;
* permanent institutions;
* universal political systems;
* universal objective functions.

Such assumptions may be introduced within specific applications but are not part of the foundational architecture.

# Criteria for Revision

Because the axioms constitute the theoretical foundation, revisions should occur only when at least one of the following conditions is satisfied.

1. Internal logical inconsistency.
2. Persistent inability to explain important classes of social evolution.
3. Discovery of a more fundamental assumption from which one or more existing axioms can be derived.
4. Empirical evidence demonstrating that one axiom is unnecessary across broad classes of applications.

Routine refinement of mechanisms, state variables, or mathematical models should not alter the axioms.

---

# Working Principle

The purpose of these axioms is not to explain every aspect of social life directly.

Their purpose is to define the smallest set of primitive assumptions from which a coherent and empirically testable theory of social-system evolution can be constructed.

Accordingly, every subsequent extension of the framework should be evaluated against a simple question:

> **Does this proposal require changing the axioms, or can it be derived from them?**

Only proposals that satisfy the first condition should motivate reconsideration of the theoretical foundation.

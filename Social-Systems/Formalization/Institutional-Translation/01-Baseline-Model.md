# Baseline Model

## A Minimal Formal Representation of Institutional Translation

**Repository**

`Research-Lab/Social-Systems/Formalization/Institutional-Translation/01-Baseline-Model.md`

**Status**

Working Draft

**Phase**

VI — Formalization

**Purpose**

To construct the smallest formal model capable of distinguishing Institutional Translation from actor-characteristic effects, direct institutional effects, and unexplained outcome variation.

---

# 1. Purpose of the Baseline Model

Institutional Translation proposes that institutions do more than affect outcomes directly.

Institutions condition how actor characteristics are converted into social outcomes.

The central question is:

> Does the effect of an actor characteristic change systematically with the institutional environment?

The baseline model isolates this claim using:

* one actor characteristic;
* one institutional dimension;
* one observable outcome;
* one translation term;
* one stochastic disturbance.

The model intentionally excludes:

* institutional evolution;
* political feedback;
* network effects;
* behavioral adaptation;
* development–allocation co-evolution;
* multiple institutional levels;
* strategic institutional choice.

These elements may matter in later models, but they are not required to define the baseline translation mechanism.

---

# 2. System Boundary

Consider a bounded social system containing actors:

[
i=1,\ldots,n
]

observed over periods:

[
t=0,1,2,\ldots
]

The relevant institutional environment is shared by actors within the selected system and period.

The system may represent:

* a labor market;
* an educational system;
* a jurisdiction;
* an organization;
* a credit market;
* an electoral system;
* another institutionally bounded domain.

A concrete application must identify the boundary before assigning substantive meaning to the variables.

---

# 3. Minimum State Representation

The minimum state vector is:

[
S_t^{IT}
========

(H_t,I_t,E_t),
]

where:

* (H_t) represents the distribution of the selected actor characteristic;
* (I_t) represents the selected institutional condition;
* (E_t) represents relevant external conditions.

At the actor level:

[
H_{i,t}=h_{i,t},
]

where (h_{i,t}) is initially scalar.

The model generates the observable outcome:

[
Y_{i,t+1}.
]

The outcome is not included as a predetermined state variable in the baseline model. It is generated by the translation process.

---

# 4. Variables

## Actor Characteristic

Let:

[
h_{i,t}\in\mathcal{H}
]

represent one characteristic of actor (i).

Possible interpretations include:

* education;
* skill;
* wealth;
* health;
* legal status;
* social position;
* geographic location;
* network access.

Only one interpretation should be used in any baseline application.

---

## Institutional Condition

Let:

[
I_t\in\mathcal{I}
]

represent one institutional dimension.

It may be:

### Binary

[
I_t\in{0,1},
]

representing two institutional regimes.

### Categorical

[
I_t\in{1,\ldots,R},
]

representing multiple regimes.

### Continuous

[
I_t\in\mathbb{R},
]

representing institutional strength, enforcement, accessibility, or restrictiveness.

The baseline derivation begins with a scalar institutional variable.

---

## Observable Outcome

Let:

[
Y_{i,t+1}\in\mathbb{R}
]

represent the outcome generated after the institutional translation of (h_{i,t}).

Possible outcomes include:

* earnings;
* educational attainment;
* promotion;
* credit access;
* asset accumulation;
* political influence;
* occupational entry;
* exposure to risk.

---

## External Conditions

Let:

[
E_t
]

represent observed environmental conditions relevant to the outcome.

The baseline model may omit (E_t) from the displayed equations when the environment is held constant or absorbed into fixed effects.

---

## Stochastic Disturbance

Let:

[
\varepsilon_{i,t+1}
]

represent unobserved influences, measurement error, and stochastic contingency.

Assume initially:

[
\mathbb{E}
\left[
\varepsilon_{i,t+1}
\mid
h_{i,t},I_t,E_t
\right]
=======

0.

]

This is a simplifying identification assumption, not a general property of social systems.

---

# 5. Benchmark Model 1 — Actor Characteristic Only

The simplest benchmark is:

[
Y_{i,t+1}
=========

\alpha
+
\beta h_{i,t}
+
\varepsilon_{i,t+1}.
]

Here:

* (\alpha) is the baseline outcome;
* (\beta) is the return to the actor characteristic.

The marginal effect of the characteristic is:

[
\frac{\partial Y_{i,t+1}}{\partial h_{i,t}}
===========================================

\beta.
]

The effect is assumed to be the same across institutional environments.

This model resembles explanations in which outcomes are determined primarily by actor capabilities, resources, or other individual characteristics.

---

# 6. Benchmark Model 2 — Additive Institutional Effect

The second benchmark adds institutions directly:

[
Y_{i,t+1}
=========

\alpha
+
\beta h_{i,t}
+
\gamma I_t
+
\varepsilon_{i,t+1}.
]

Here:

* (\beta) remains the return to the actor characteristic;
* (\gamma) is the direct institutional effect.

The marginal effects are:

[
\frac{\partial Y_{i,t+1}}{\partial h_{i,t}}
===========================================

\beta
]

and

[
\frac{\partial Y_{i,t+1}}{\partial I_t}
=======================================

\gamma.
]

Institutions shift expected outcomes, but they do not change the return to the actor characteristic.

This model recognizes institutional importance without representing Institutional Translation.

---

# 7. Baseline Institutional Translation Model

The baseline model is:

[
Y_{i,t+1}
=========

\alpha
+
\beta h_{i,t}
+
\gamma I_t
+
\delta h_{i,t}I_t
+
\lambda^{\top}E_t
+
\varepsilon_{i,t+1}.
]

The parameters have the following meanings:

| Parameter | Interpretation                                                          |
| --------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| (\alpha)  | Baseline expected outcome                                               |
| (\beta)   | Effect of the actor characteristic at the institutional reference value |
| (\gamma)  | Direct institutional effect at the characteristic reference value       |
| (\delta)  | Institutional Translation effect                                        |
| (\lambda) | Effects of observed external conditions                                 |

The translation term is:

[
\delta h_{i,t}I_t.
]

Institutional Translation is present when:

[
\delta\neq 0.
]

---

# 8. Marginal Interpretation

The effect of the actor characteristic is:

[
\frac{\partial Y_{i,t+1}}{\partial h_{i,t}}
===========================================

\beta+\delta I_t.
]

Therefore, the return to (h_{i,t}) depends on the institutional condition.

Similarly, the effect of institutions is:

[
\frac{\partial Y_{i,t+1}}{\partial I_t}
=======================================

\gamma+\delta h_{i,t}.
]

Therefore, institutional effects differ across actors with different characteristics.

The cross-partial derivative is:

[
\frac{\partial^2Y_{i,t+1}}
{\partial h_{i,t}\partial I_t}
==============================

\delta.
]

This cross-partial is the baseline mathematical representation of Institutional Translation.

---

# 9. Binary Institutional Regime

Suppose:

[
I_t\in{0,1}.
]

Under the reference regime:

[
I_t=0,
]

the expected outcome is:

[
\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t+1}\mid h_{i,t},I_t=0]
=======================================

\alpha+\beta h_{i,t}.
]

Under the alternative regime:

[
I_t=1,
]

the expected outcome is:

[
\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t+1}\mid h_{i,t},I_t=1]
=======================================

(\alpha+\gamma)
+
(\beta+\delta)h_{i,t}.
]

The institutional change can therefore affect:

* the intercept through (\gamma);
* the slope through (\delta).

The direct institutional effect changes the general outcome level.

The translation effect changes how the actor characteristic is rewarded, penalized, or otherwise converted into an outcome.

---

# 10. Institutional Translation versus Standard Interaction

Mathematically, the baseline translation term is an interaction.

That alone does not establish a theoretical contribution.

Institutional Translation adds analytical value only when the interaction has a defined mechanism.

A valid translation claim should identify:

1. the actor characteristic entering the process;
2. the institutional rule or arrangement;
3. the decision, eligibility, enforcement, or assignment process through which translation occurs;
4. the resulting outcome;
5. why the institutional arrangement changes the effect of the actor characteristic.

Without this mechanism, the term:

[
h_{i,t}I_t
]

is merely a statistical interaction.

The theoretical content comes from the institutional process represented by the interaction.

---

# 11. A Mechanism-Based Interpretation

Suppose the outcome is generated through an intermediate opportunity:

[
O_{i,t}
=======

\Omega(h_{i,t},I_t),
]

where (O_{i,t}) may represent:

* eligibility;
* access;
* assignment;
* authorization;
* exposure;
* institutional recognition.

The final outcome is:

[
Y_{i,t+1}
=========

\Gamma(O_{i,t},h_{i,t},E_t)
+
\varepsilon_{i,t+1}.
]

The full sequence is:

[
h_{i,t}
\overset{I_t}{\longrightarrow}
O_{i,t}
\longrightarrow
Y_{i,t+1}.
]

This formulation makes the translation mechanism explicit.

Institutions affect the intermediate opportunity or constraint through which the characteristic influences the final outcome.

---

# 12. Reduced Opportunity Model

A minimal opportunity equation is:

[
O_{i,t}
=======

a
+
b h_{i,t}
+
c I_t
+
d h_{i,t}I_t
+
u_{i,t}.
]

The outcome equation is:

[
Y_{i,t+1}
=========

q
+
r O_{i,t}
+
s h_{i,t}
+
v_{i,t+1}.
]

Substituting the opportunity equation gives:

[
Y_{i,t+1}
=========

(q+ra)
+
(rb+s)h_{i,t}
+
rcI_t
+
rdh_{i,t}I_t
+
ru_{i,t}
+
v_{i,t+1}.
]

The reduced-form translation coefficient is:

[
\delta=rd.
]

This decomposition clarifies that Institutional Translation may operate through an identifiable intermediate institutional allocation process.

---

# 13. Translation Channels

The baseline model recognizes several possible channels.

## Eligibility Channel

Institutions determine whether actors qualify for an opportunity.

## Access Channel

Institutions determine whether qualified actors can reach or use the opportunity.

## Assignment Channel

Institutions assign actors to positions, schools, occupations, benefits, or jurisdictions.

## Enforcement Channel

Institutions determine whether formal rights and rules are implemented.

## Reward Channel

Institutions determine the return associated with a characteristic or position.

## Risk Channel

Institutions determine how exposure to loss, punishment, liability, or uncertainty is allocated.

A baseline application should select one primary channel.

---

# 14. Direct and Indirect Translation

Institutional Translation may operate directly:

[
I_t
\rightarrow
\Phi
\rightarrow
Y_{i,t+1},
]

or indirectly through an intermediate institutional outcome:

[
I_t
\rightarrow
O_{i,t}
\rightarrow
Y_{i,t+1}.
]

The baseline empirical specification may estimate the reduced form first.

Mechanism validation should then determine whether the proposed intermediate channel is observable.

---

# 15. Distributional Consequences

For a population with characteristic distribution:

[
h_{i,t}\sim F_{H,t},
]

the institutional regime generates an outcome distribution:

[
Y_{i,t+1}
=========

\alpha
+
\beta h_{i,t}
+
\gamma I_t
+
\delta h_{i,t}I_t
+
\varepsilon_{i,t+1}.
]

The expected outcome is:

[
\mathbb{E}[Y_{t+1}\mid I_t]
===========================

\alpha
+
\gamma I_t
+
(\beta+\delta I_t)\mathbb{E}[H_t].
]

Assuming the disturbance is independent of (H_t), the conditional variance is:

[
\operatorname{Var}(Y_{t+1}\mid I_t)
===================================

(\beta+\delta I_t)^2
\operatorname{Var}(H_t)
+
\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_{t+1}).
]

Institutions may therefore change both:

* average outcomes;
* outcome dispersion.

This can occur even when the distribution of actor characteristics remains unchanged.

---

# 16. Institutional Compression and Amplification

Institutional Translation may compress or amplify differences.

## Amplifying Translation

Institutions amplify characteristic differences when:

[
\left|\beta+\delta I_t\right|

>

|\beta|.
]

## Compressing Translation

Institutions compress characteristic differences when:

[
\left|\beta+\delta I_t\right|
<
|\beta|.
]

## Neutral Translation

Institutions leave the characteristic gradient unchanged when:

[
\delta=0.
]

These concepts describe effects on the translation slope.

They do not by themselves determine whether the institutional regime is normatively desirable.

---

# 17. Institutional Reversal

A sufficiently strong translation effect may reverse the relationship between a characteristic and an outcome.

A reversal occurs when:

[
\beta
\left(
\beta+\delta I_t
\right)
<0.
]

For example, a characteristic associated with advantage in one institutional regime may become neutral or disadvantageous in another.

Such reversals should be treated as strong empirical claims requiring substantial evidence.

---

# 18. Testable Propositions

## Proposition 1 — Conditional Return

The marginal effect of the actor characteristic varies with the institutional regime:

[
\frac{\partial Y}{\partial h}
=============================

\beta+\delta I.
]

---

## Proposition 2 — Institutional Heterogeneity of Outcomes

Actors with different characteristics experience different effects from the same institutional change:

[
\frac{\partial Y}{\partial I}
=============================

\gamma+\delta h.
]

---

## Proposition 3 — Allocation Change without Capability Change

A change in (I_t) can alter the outcome distribution even when:

[
F_{H,t+1}
\approx
F_{H,t}.
]

---

## Proposition 4 — Compression or Amplification

Institutional change may increase or decrease the dispersion generated by actor heterogeneity.

---

## Proposition 5 — Implementation Dependence

Where formal institutional change does not alter effective institutional practice, the translation coefficient should remain substantially unchanged.

---

# 19. Null Hypotheses

The baseline model produces several testable null hypotheses.

## No Institutional Effect

[
H_0:
\gamma=0
\quad\text{and}\quad
\delta=0.
]

Institutions neither shift outcomes nor alter the characteristic gradient.

## Additive Institutions Only

[
H_0:
\delta=0.
]

Institutions affect outcomes directly but do not translate actor characteristics differently.

## Translation without Direct Shift

[
H_0:
\gamma=0,
\qquad
\delta\neq 0.
]

Institutions change the characteristic gradient without changing the reference-group outcome.

The core Institutional Translation test is:

[
H_0:\delta=0.
]

---

# 20. Comparative Evaluation

The models should be compared in sequence.

## Model A — Characteristic Only

[
Y=\alpha+\beta h+\varepsilon.
]

## Model B — Additive Institution

[
Y=\alpha+\beta h+\gamma I+\varepsilon.
]

## Model C — Institutional Translation

[
Y=\alpha+\beta h+\gamma I+\delta hI+\varepsilon.
]

The translation model earns its added complexity only if it provides:

* improved explanatory fit;
* better out-of-sample prediction;
* theoretically meaningful heterogeneity;
* stronger counterfactual interpretation;
* identifiable mechanism evidence.

Statistical significance alone is insufficient.

---

# 21. Counterfactual Translation

Holding actor characteristics constant, the counterfactual outcome under institutional regime (I^{CF}) is:

[
Y_{i,t+1}^{CF}
==============

\alpha
+
\beta h_{i,t}
+
\gamma I^{CF}
+
\delta h_{i,t}I^{CF}.
]

The actor-level institutional effect is:

[
\Delta Y_i
==========

Y_i^{CF}-Y_i^{Observed}.
]

For observed regime (I^O):

[
\Delta Y_i
==========

\gamma(I^{CF}-I^O)
+
\delta h_i(I^{CF}-I^O).
]

The counterfactual effect differs across actors whenever:

[
\delta\neq 0.
]

This is a central consequence of Institutional Translation.

---

# 22. Identification Requirements

A credible empirical test requires variation in:

* actor characteristics;
* institutional conditions;
* outcomes.

It also requires a strategy for addressing:

* institutional endogeneity;
* actor sorting;
* omitted institutional dimensions;
* differential measurement;
* temporal sequencing;
* correlated shocks;
* formal versus effective institutions.

Potential identification strategies include:

* institutional reform;
* jurisdictional boundaries;
* cohort exposure;
* randomized institutional procedures;
* phased implementation;
* eligibility thresholds;
* natural experiments;
* matched comparative cases.

---

# 23. Formal versus Effective Institutions

Let:

[
I_t^F
]

represent formal institutional rules and:

[
I_t^E
]

represent effective implementation.

The outcome model may be written:

[
Y_{i,t+1}
=========

\alpha
+
\beta h_{i,t}
+
\gamma_F I_t^F
+
\gamma_E I_t^E
+
\delta_F h_{i,t}I_t^F
+
\delta_E h_{i,t}I_t^E
+
\varepsilon_{i,t+1}.
]

A formal reform may fail to change outcomes when:

[
I_t^F\neq I_{t-1}^F
]

but:

[
I_t^E\approx I_{t-1}^E.
]

The baseline model begins with one institutional measure, but later applications should distinguish formal rules from effective practice when necessary.

---

# 24. Scope Conditions

The baseline model is most appropriate when:

* one institutional dimension is substantively central;
* actor characteristics vary within the system;
* institutional regimes vary across systems or periods;
* the outcome is observable;
* translation occurs over a defined time interval;
* the mechanism can be linked to eligibility, access, assignment, enforcement, reward, or risk.

It is less suitable when:

* institutions are nearly constant;
* actor sorting cannot be addressed;
* the institutional dimension is poorly measured;
* outcomes are determined mainly by physical constraints;
* multiple institutional mechanisms cannot be separated;
* the system boundary is unclear.

---

# 25. Falsification Criteria

The baseline translation claim should be weakened if:

* (\delta) is repeatedly indistinguishable from zero in settings with substantial institutional variation;
* estimated translation effects are unstable across reasonable specifications;
* actor sorting fully explains the apparent interaction;
* institutional reform changes neither the relevant intermediate opportunity nor the final outcome gradient;
* additive benchmark models perform equally well;
* the proposed institutional channel cannot be identified;
* the sign and meaning of the translation effect vary without defensible scope conditions.

The strong claim should be rejected within the selected domain if institutions do not materially alter the mapping from the actor characteristic to the outcome.

---

# 26. What the Baseline Model Does Not Establish

Even when:

[
\delta\neq 0,
]

the model does not by itself establish:

* causality;
* institutional optimality;
* normative desirability;
* institutional evolution;
* recursive feedback;
* generality across domains;
* superiority of the full Dynamic Social Systems framework.

Those claims require additional evidence and later models.

---

# 27. Positive–Normative Separation

Suppose one institution compresses outcome differences.

That does not automatically mean it increases welfare.

A normative judgment requires an explicit evaluative function:

[
W(A_{t+1},D_{t+1},\ldots).
]

The baseline model describes how institutions alter outcomes.

It does not determine which translation rule society should prefer.

---

# 28. First Application Standard

The first substantive application should specify:

1. the bounded institutional domain;
2. one actor characteristic;
3. one institutional variable;
4. one observable outcome;
5. the proposed translation channel;
6. the additive benchmark;
7. the expected sign of (\delta);
8. the identification strategy;
9. the falsification condition.

The application should be chosen for analytical clarity rather than social importance alone.

* (I): property-right, tax, or credit regime;

# 31. Development Judgment

The baseline model successfully provides a minimal formal definition of Institutional Translation.

It distinguishes:

* actor-characteristic effects;
* direct institutional effects;
* conditional translation effects;
* stochastic variation.

Its central identifying feature is:

[
\frac{\partial^2Y}
{\partial h,\partial I}
\neq 0.
]

However, this mathematical condition is not sufficient by itself.

A substantive Institutional Translation model also requires an identifiable institutional channel connecting the characteristic to the outcome.

The baseline is therefore a valid starting point, not a completed theory.

# Falsification Criteria

## Conditions for Revising or Rejecting the Dynamic Social Systems Framework

**Repository**

`Research-Lab/Social-Systems/Validation/05-Falsification-Criteria.md`

**Status**

Working Draft

**Phase**

V — Framework Validation

**Purpose**

To specify the evidence, observations, and comparative results that would weaken, revise, or reject the major propositions of the Dynamic Social Systems framework, and to distinguish failures of individual propositions from failures of the framework as a whole.

---

# 1. Purpose of Falsification Criteria

The Dynamic Social Systems framework should not be considered scientifically useful merely because it can accommodate many forms of social change.

A framework that can explain every possible outcome after the fact cannot discriminate among competing explanations.

The preceding validation document identified testable propositions concerning:

* Institutional Translation;
* recursive feedback;
* persistence;
* institutional adaptive capacity;
* endogenous transition dynamics;
* political feedback;
* development–allocation co-evolution;
* declining intervention effectiveness;
* adaptation-speed mismatch;
* role-dependent decision-making;
* contingency;
* network amplification;
* institutional rigidity;
* legitimacy;
* survivorship and selection.

The present document asks:

> What evidence would show that these propositions are incorrect, overstated, unnecessary, or insufficiently distinct from existing theories?

Falsification does not require that every failed prediction invalidate the entire framework.

Instead, failures may imply that:

* a proposition should be rejected;
* a mechanism should be narrowed;
* a boundary condition is missing;
* a state variable is unnecessary;
* an alternative theory explains the evidence better;
* or the framework lacks sufficient explanatory content.

---

# 2. Levels of Falsification

The framework should be evaluated at several levels.

## A. Proposition-Level Falsification

A specific predicted relationship repeatedly fails to appear under the conditions in which it should hold.

---

## B. Mechanism-Level Falsification

The observable outcome occurs, but evidence does not support the proposed mechanism generating it.

---

## C. Architectural Falsification

The distinctions among states, mechanisms, observables, and evaluative mappings do not improve explanation or model construction.

---

## D. Comparative Falsification

A simpler or established theory explains the same evidence equally well or better with fewer assumptions.

---

## E. Framework-Level Falsification

The central architecture fails to generate distinctive, accurate, or transferable explanations across applications.

---

# 3. General Standards

A claim should be weakened or rejected when one or more of the following conditions hold.

## Persistent Null Findings

Repeated well-designed studies find no relationship where the framework predicts one.

---

## Opposite-Signed Findings

Evidence consistently shows effects in the opposite direction from the proposition.

---

## Lack of Mechanism Evidence

The predicted outcome appears, but the proposed institutional, behavioral, or feedback process is absent.

---

## Instability across Reasonable Specifications

The result disappears under modest changes in measurement, model specification, sample, or historical period.

---

## Superior Alternative Explanation

A simpler theory explains the evidence with equal or greater accuracy and fewer unobserved components.

---

## Unbounded Flexibility

The framework can be adjusted after every result without changing any core claim.

---

## Failure of Portability

Concepts that are claimed to be general require entirely different meanings across applications.

---

# 4. Falsification of Institutional Translation

## Proposition

The effect of a given characteristic on social outcomes varies systematically across institutional environments.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* the return to relevant characteristics remains stable across substantially different institutional regimes;
* institutional interaction effects are consistently small or statistically indistinguishable from zero;
* observed outcome differences are explained fully by capability differences, selection, or measurement;
* formal institutional reforms do not alter the mapping from characteristics to outcomes;
* institutional variables add little explanatory power once conventional controls are included.

## Evidence That Would Reject the Strong Form

The strong form should be rejected if repeated comparative studies show that institutional variation does not materially alter the relationship between actor characteristics and outcomes.

## Possible Revision

Institutional Translation may need to be restricted to particular domains, institutions, or forms of heterogeneity.

---

# 5. Falsification of Allocation Change through Institutional Translation

## Proposition

Institutional change can alter allocation without major prior changes in underlying capabilities.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* allocation changes only after measurable changes in productivity or capabilities;
* institutional reforms produce no distributional change;
* apparent effects disappear after accounting for compositional change;
* differences are temporary and do not affect longer-term allocation;
* reforms change formal rules but not effective institutional practice.

## Possible Revision

The claim may need to distinguish formal reform from implemented institutional change.

---

# 6. Falsification of Recursive Outcome Feedback

## Proposition

Current social outcomes reshape future institutions, capabilities, preferences, and opportunities.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* current outcomes do not predict future opportunities after initial conditions are controlled;
* institutional and behavioral states remain unaffected by prior outcomes;
* persistence results entirely from fixed exogenous characteristics;
* feedback pathways cannot be identified through process tracing or temporal ordering;
* interventions that alter outcomes do not change subsequent trajectories.

## Evidence That Would Reject the Strong Form

The strong form should be rejected if outcomes consistently operate only as endpoints and do not become causal inputs into later transitions.

---

# 7. Falsification of Feedback Strength and Persistence

## Proposition

Stronger recursive feedback increases persistence and reduces mobility, unless offset by corrective forces.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* systems with stronger outcome-to-opportunity links display greater mobility;
* feedback strength does not predict persistence;
* persistence remains unchanged after major feedback channels are disrupted;
* corrective institutions are unnecessary to explain high mobility;
* observed persistence is attributable entirely to inherited traits or stable preferences.

## Possible Revision

The relationship may be nonlinear or depend on whether feedback is reinforcing or compensatory.

---

# 8. Falsification of Institutional Adaptive Capacity

## Proposition

Systems with greater adaptive capacity respond faster and more effectively to failures and shocks.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* measured adaptive capacity does not predict response speed;
* rapid institutional response produces no better recovery;
* rigid systems perform as well as flexible systems after comparable shocks;
* formal revision procedures do not translate into implemented change;
* adaptation produces instability or repeated policy error rather than correction.

## Evidence That Would Reject the Strong Form

The strong form should be rejected if institutional flexibility has no systematic relationship with correction, recovery, or reduced mismatch.

## Possible Revision

Adaptive capacity may need to be decomposed into:

* detection;
* decision;
* implementation;
* enforcement;
* learning;
* legitimacy.

---

# 9. Falsification of Adaptive Capacity as a Moderator of Path Dependence

## Proposition

Institutional adaptive capacity weakens the long-run effect of adverse historical conditions.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* historically disadvantaged systems remain equally constrained despite high adaptive capacity;
* low-capacity systems escape historical constraints as frequently as high-capacity systems;
* observed divergence is explained fully by external resources or shocks;
* institutional reform does not reduce inherited persistence;
* adaptive institutions reproduce rather than correct prior inequalities.

## Possible Revision

Adaptive capacity may moderate only some forms of path dependence and may itself be historically endogenous.

---

# 10. Falsification of Endogenous Transition Dynamics

## Proposition

Transition probabilities change as institutions, behavior, and political conditions change.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* transition matrices remain stable across major reforms;
* mobility and persistence parameters do not vary across institutional regimes;
* cohort transitions remain unchanged despite meaningful policy variation;
* transition changes are explained fully by compositional shifts;
* institutional variables do not improve forecasts of future movement.

## Evidence That Would Reject the Strong Form

The strong form should be rejected if transition probabilities behave as stable structural parameters across substantially different institutional conditions.

---

# 11. Falsification of Political Feedback

## Proposition

Distributional outcomes affect future transition dynamics through political power, policy, and institutional reform.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* concentration does not predict later institutional or policy change;
* political influence remains unrelated to resource distribution;
* reforms occur independently of changes in distribution;
* institutional responses are explained better by ideology, shocks, or external pressure;
* resource-rich groups fail to convert economic position into political influence.

## Possible Revision

Political feedback may depend strongly on electoral rules, organization, state capacity, media systems, and collective-action conditions.

---

# 12. Falsification of Development–Allocation Co-evolution

## Proposition

Development and allocation affect one another dynamically and conditionally.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* development and allocation evolve independently over long periods;
* lagged allocation does not predict later development;
* lagged development does not predict later allocation;
* institutions do not condition the relationship;
* a stable universal relationship explains the evidence better than regime-dependent effects.

## Evidence That Would Reject the Strong Form

The strong form should be rejected if neither direction of influence is consistently observable and the two dimensions can be modeled independently without explanatory loss.

## Possible Revision

Co-evolution may apply only through specific mechanisms such as education, political stability, investment, or fiscal capacity.

---

# 13. Falsification of Institutional Regime Dependence

## Proposition

The effect of an intervention depends on the institutional regime in which it operates.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* identical interventions produce stable effects across sharply different institutional contexts;
* institutional interactions are consistently negligible;
* implementation capacity does not alter treatment effects;
* differences are explained entirely by population composition;
* institutional context adds no predictive value.

## Possible Revision

Regime dependence may apply only to interventions requiring discretion, enforcement, coordination, or behavioral compliance.

---

# 14. Falsification of Declining Intervention Effectiveness

## Proposition

Intervention effectiveness declines when actors adapt strategically and institutions fail to revise accordingly.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* program effects remain stable despite opportunities for adaptation;
* behavioral change strengthens rather than weakens effectiveness;
* declining effects are explained fully by funding reductions or implementation failure;
* actors do not alter behavior in response to the intervention;
* institutional revision does not restore effectiveness.

## Evidence That Would Reject the Strong Form

The strong form should be rejected if strategic adaptation rarely affects policy effectiveness or if observed declines do not follow the predicted sequence.

## Possible Revision

The proposition may apply primarily where actors possess information, discretion, and substitution opportunities.

---

# 15. Falsification of Adaptation-Speed Mismatch

## Proposition

Institutional failure is more likely when behavioral or technological adaptation occurs faster than institutional adaptation.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* rapid behavioral change does not generate regulatory or institutional gaps;
* slow institutional adjustment does not reduce performance;
* fast institutional change performs worse than deliberate gradual adjustment;
* mismatch does not predict crisis, noncompliance, or informal substitution;
* institutions remain effective through flexible interpretation without formal revision.

## Possible Revision

The relevant variable may be not absolute speed but the compatibility between adaptation rates.

---

# 16. Falsification of Role-Dependent Decision-Making

## Proposition

Individual choices vary systematically with the role made salient by the decision context.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* role primes do not alter preferences or choices;
* within-person decisions remain stable across contexts;
* apparent role effects disappear after accounting for material incentives;
* individuals consistently apply one preference ordering;
* role salience adds no explanatory power beyond identity or framing variables.

## Evidence That Would Reject the Strong Form

The strong form should be rejected if decisions are better explained by stable preferences and changing constraints than by role activation.

## Possible Revision

Role dependence may be limited to political, moral, or institutionally ambiguous decisions.

---

# 17. Falsification of Preference Aggregation Loss

## Proposition

Institutional outcomes diverge from underlying individual preferences because preferences are transformed through multiple aggregation stages.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* expressed preferences reliably predict collective outcomes;
* institutional rules do not affect aggregation results;
* intrapersonal and interpersonal aggregation add no explanatory value;
* policy outcomes closely track stable median preferences;
* representation gaps are random rather than systematic.

## Possible Revision

Aggregation loss may be relevant only under specific agenda-setting, voting, representation, or information conditions.

---

# 18. Falsification of Contingency and Divergence

## Proposition

Small or temporary shocks can produce large long-run divergence when feedback and path dependence are strong.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* similar systems consistently return to their prior trajectories after shocks;
* temporary shocks leave no durable effects;
* divergence is explained entirely by persistent structural differences;
* timing has little effect on long-run outcomes;
* institutional state does not condition shock persistence.

## Possible Revision

The proposition may require identifiable threshold conditions or critical periods.

---

# 19. Falsification of Unequal Shock Amplification

## Proposition

Common shocks produce unequal long-run effects because actors differ in exposure, buffers, access, and recovery capacity.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* recovery is uniform across social positions;
* initial buffers do not predict later outcomes;
* institutional access does not affect recovery;
* short-run inequalities disappear quickly;
* differential outcomes are explained entirely by voluntary choice.

## Evidence That Would Reject the Strong Form

The strong form should be rejected if broadly shared shocks do not systematically interact with preexisting heterogeneity.

---

# 20. Falsification of Network Amplification

## Proposition

Networks magnify initial differences when access to opportunities depends on reinforcing connections.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* network position does not predict outcomes after individual characteristics are controlled;
* institutional standardization does not reduce network effects;
* network advantages do not accumulate;
* exclusion from high-value networks has little long-run effect;
* observed clustering reflects sorting rather than amplification.

## Possible Revision

The mechanism may operate only in domains where information, referrals, trust, or gatekeeping are central.

---

# 21. Falsification of Corrective Institutions and Early Intervention

## Proposition

Corrective institutions reduce cumulative advantage more effectively when intervention occurs before early differences become embedded.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* early intervention has no greater long-run effect than later intervention;
* timing does not interact with feedback strength;
* cumulative advantage remains unchanged after early support;
* late reforms reverse embedded disadvantage equally well;
* observed timing effects are explained entirely by program quality.

## Possible Revision

Timing may matter only for particular capabilities, developmental stages, or institutional pathways.

---

# 22. Falsification of Development without Allocation Improvement

## Proposition

Development may rise while allocation remains unchanged or worsens when institutions channel gains toward initially advantaged actors.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* development gains consistently improve allocation;
* institutional concentration does not affect the distribution of gains;
* median and lower-group outcomes rise proportionately with aggregate development;
* rising concentration is explained entirely by temporary transitions;
* allocation changes independently of institutional channels.

## Possible Revision

The proposition may apply primarily to capital-intensive, network-intensive, or politically concentrated development regimes.

---

# 23. Falsification of Joint Development and Allocation Improvement

## Proposition

Some institutional reforms improve both development and allocation by expanding capabilities or removing barriers.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* allocation improvements consistently reduce development;
* barrier removal produces no aggregate gain;
* expanded participation does not affect productivity or innovation;
* apparent joint improvements arise from external shocks;
* effects disappear after implementation costs are included.

## Possible Revision

Joint improvement may require unused capability, exclusionary barriers, and adequate implementation capacity.

---

# 24. Falsification of Institutional Rigidity and Regime Transition

## Proposition

Rigid systems facing accumulating mismatch are more likely to change abruptly than gradually.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* rigid institutions adjust incrementally despite prolonged pressure;
* abrupt transitions occur equally often in flexible systems;
* accumulated mismatch does not predict institutional discontinuity;
* regime changes are explained entirely by external shocks;
* institutional rigidity does not increase adjustment costs.

## Possible Revision

Abrupt transition may require both rigidity and a threshold-crossing shock.

---

# 25. Falsification of Institutional Legitimacy Feedback

## Proposition

Persistent divergence between institutional promises and outcomes reduces legitimacy and affects compliance, participation, and stability.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* poor performance does not reduce trust or legitimacy;
* legitimacy changes do not affect behavior;
* compliance remains stable despite legitimacy decline;
* ideology or coercion fully explains institutional persistence;
* performance improvements do not restore legitimacy.

## Possible Revision

Legitimacy feedback may depend on information, attribution, group identity, and credible alternatives.

---

# 26. Falsification of Survivorship and Selection Bias

## Proposition

Observed institutions and strategies disproportionately reflect survivors rather than universally optimal alternatives.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* surviving institutions consistently outperform plausible alternatives under varied conditions;
* failed alternatives leave no important missing evidence;
* selection processes reliably identify general efficiency;
* historical contingency contributes little to survival;
* success remains robust outside the original environment.

## Possible Revision

Survivorship bias may be strongest where failed alternatives disappear from records or where selection occurs under narrow historical conditions.

---

# 27. Falsification of Cross-Domain Portability

## Proposition

A stable core architecture can explain structurally similar transition patterns across multiple substantive domains.

## Evidence That Would Weaken the Proposition

The proposition would be weakened if:

* core concepts require different definitions in every application;
* no common mechanism structure survives across domains;
* the framework adds no value beyond domain-specific theories;
* applications rely on unrelated state variables and causal assumptions;
* portability is achieved only by increasing abstraction until the framework becomes vacuous.

## Evidence That Would Reject the Strong Form

The strong portability claim should be rejected if the framework cannot retain stable concepts, relationships, and tests across at least several applications.

---

# 28. Falsification of the Four Foundational Axioms

The four axioms are provisional foundations rather than empirically validated laws.

They should remain open to revision.

## Axiom 1 — Heterogeneity

The axiom would be weakened as a foundational requirement if representative-agent or homogeneous models repeatedly explain the relevant dynamics without meaningful loss.

---

## Axiom 2 — Institutional Translation

The axiom would be weakened if institutional mediation adds no explanatory value beyond direct effects of capabilities, preferences, and resources.

---

## Axiom 3 — Contingency

The axiom would be weakened if historical shocks, timing, and stochastic events have negligible effects on long-run trajectories after structural conditions are considered.

---

## Axiom 4 — Recursive Feedback

The axiom would be weakened if outcomes do not systematically reshape future state variables or transition processes.

---

# 29. Framework-Level Failure Conditions

The Dynamic Social Systems framework should be substantially revised or abandoned if several of the following conditions are met.

## No Distinctive Predictions

The framework generates no predictions beyond those already produced by neighboring theories.

---

## No Comparative Advantage

Simpler theories explain the same outcomes with fewer assumptions and equal or better accuracy.

---

## No Operational Mapping

Core concepts cannot be connected to observable variables, historical evidence, or formal relationships.

---

## Unstable Definitions

Key concepts change meaning across applications.

---

## Unmanageable Complexity

The framework requires so many state variables and mechanisms that it cannot be estimated, simulated, or interpreted.

---

## Post Hoc Flexibility

Every contradictory observation can be accommodated by invoking an unspecified mechanism or boundary condition.

---

## Failure across Applications

The architecture does not transfer coherently across inequality, mobility, institutional change, or other intended domains.

---

## No Mechanism Evidence

Observed patterns are compatible with the framework, but the proposed mechanisms cannot be identified.

---

## No Added Counterfactual Value

The framework does not improve disciplined reasoning about alternative institutional arrangements or historical trajectories.

---

# 30. Criteria for Revision Rather Than Rejection

Not every failure should invalidate the framework.

Revision is appropriate when:

* one proposition fails but others remain supported;
* a relationship holds only under narrower scope conditions;
* a mechanism is valid but misclassified;
* a state variable proves unnecessary;
* an observable is measured poorly;
* an auxiliary assumption, rather than the core architecture, causes failure;
* a more precise temporal or institutional scale resolves the contradiction.

Possible revisions include:

* narrowing the applicable domain;
* separating reinforcing from corrective feedback;
* decomposing institutional adaptive capacity;
* distinguishing formal from effective institutions;
* redefining the system boundary;
* reducing the state vector;
* replacing a universal claim with a conditional one;
* abandoning a mechanism that adds no explanatory value.

---

# 31. Criteria for Strengthening Confidence

Evidence should strengthen confidence in the framework when:

* predicted institutional interactions replicate across settings;
* transition probabilities change in the predicted direction after reform;
* temporal ordering supports recursive mechanisms;
* adaptive capacity predicts differential recovery;
* feedback strength predicts persistence;
* role activation produces structured within-person variation;
* simpler theories fail to explain the same patterns;
* the architecture transfers coherently across applications;
* counterfactual predictions are supported by historical or quasi-experimental evidence.

No single successful case should validate the entire framework.

Confidence should accumulate through independent tests of multiple propositions and mechanisms.

---

# 32. Burden of Proof

The burden of proof rests with the Dynamic Social Systems framework.

It is not sufficient to show that evidence is consistent with it.

The framework must demonstrate that:

* the predicted relationship was specified before observing the evidence;
* competing explanations were considered;
* the proposed mechanism is observable or inferable;
* the result is robust;
* boundary conditions are stated;
* contradictory cases are examined;
* the architecture adds explanatory or predictive value.

---

# 33. Implications for Historical Validation

The next validation stage should not select only cases that appear consistent with the framework.

Historical validation should include:

* supportive cases;
* negative cases;
* near-comparative cases;
* cases with similar initial conditions but different outcomes;
* cases where predicted feedback failed;
* cases where institutional adaptation did not occur;
* cases where simpler explanations appear sufficient.

Historical cases should be selected to challenge the framework rather than illustrate it.

# Preliminary Conclusion

The Dynamic Social Systems framework should be judged not by the breadth of phenomena it can describe, but by whether its propositions survive serious attempts at contradiction.

The most important falsification tests concern:

* whether institutions alter the mapping from heterogeneity to outcomes;
* whether outcomes become inputs into future transitions;
* whether transition probabilities change with institutional conditions;
* whether adaptive capacity predicts differential correction;
* whether development and allocation interact conditionally;
* whether the framework outperforms simpler neighboring theories.

Failure of an individual proposition does not necessarily invalidate the entire architecture.

However, repeated failure to generate distinctive predictions, identify mechanisms, preserve stable concepts, or outperform simpler explanations would undermine the framework’s claim to constitute a separate and useful theory.

The next stage should therefore test the framework against historical evidence selected explicitly for its capacity to reveal failure, boundary conditions, and competing explanations.

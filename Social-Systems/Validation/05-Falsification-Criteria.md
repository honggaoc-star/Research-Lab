# 05. Falsification Criteria

## Purpose

Scientific theories should not only explain observed phenomena but also specify the conditions under which they would be considered inconsistent with evidence.

The purpose of this document is to establish explicit falsification criteria for the Social Systems Framework.

Rather than assuming that disagreement between theory and observation necessarily invalidates the framework, this document distinguishes different sources of disagreement and specifies how each should be interpreted.

The objective is to promote scientific rigor while supporting cumulative refinement rather than premature rejection.

---

# 1. Principles of Falsification

The Social Systems Framework adopts five principles.

## Testability

Every major proposition should be capable of empirical evaluation.

---

## Transparency

Conditions that would challenge the framework should be explicitly documented before empirical testing.

---

## Proportionality

Evidence should be interpreted according to its quality, reliability, and relevance.

Extraordinary conclusions require correspondingly strong evidence.

---

## Cumulative Evaluation

No single empirical observation should automatically determine the validity of the entire framework.

Confidence should increase or decrease through the accumulation of independent evidence.

---

## Progressive Refinement

Scientific progress should occur through continuous improvement of the framework rather than protection from criticism.

---

# 2. Sources of Apparent Contradiction

Observed disagreement between theory and evidence may arise from several causes.

Distinguishing among them is essential.

---

## Measurement Failure

Observed disagreement may result from poor measurement rather than incorrect theory.

Examples include:

- inappropriate proxy variables,
- unreliable datasets,
- inconsistent statistical definitions,
- missing observations,
- survey bias.

Response:

Improve measurement before revising theory.

---

## Specification Failure

The theoretical mechanism may be incomplete.

Examples include:

- omitted variables,
- oversimplified relationships,
- incorrect functional assumptions,
- inappropriate aggregation.

Response:

Revise the formal specification while preserving validated components where appropriate.

---

## Missing Mechanisms

Observed behavior may reflect mechanisms not currently represented.

Examples include:

- technological disruption,
- cultural evolution,
- international spillovers,
- network effects,
- institutional interactions.

Response:

Expand the framework if independent evidence consistently identifies missing dynamics.

---

## Model Failure

Empirical observations may directly contradict the framework's core predictions despite reliable measurement and appropriate specification.

This represents the strongest form of falsification.

Response:

Reconsider the underlying theoretical assumptions.

---

# 3. Levels of Falsification

Not all contradictions have equal implications.

## Level I

Question individual parameter estimates.

---

## Level II

Question specific transition mechanisms.

---

## Level III

Question subsystem interactions.

---

## Level IV

Question the integrated architecture.

---

## Level V

Question the foundational assumptions of the framework.

Higher levels require substantially stronger and more consistent evidence.

---

# 4. Proposition-Based Evaluation

Each proposition defined in **04-Testable-Propositions.md** should be evaluated independently.

Possible outcomes include:

- Supported
- Partially Supported
- Inconclusive
- Challenged
- Rejected

Individual propositions may be revised without requiring rejection of the entire framework.

---

# 5. Framework-Level Evaluation

Beyond evaluating individual propositions, the integrated framework should be assessed according to broader scientific criteria.

Questions include:

- Does the framework remain internally consistent?
- Does it organize existing knowledge coherently?
- Does it improve explanatory power?
- Does it generate useful predictions?
- Does it remain empirically testable?

Failure in one component does not necessarily invalidate the complete architecture.

---

# 6. Historical Falsification

Historical evidence provides particularly valuable opportunities for evaluating long-run dynamics.

Examples include:

- institutional transitions,
- economic transformation,
- demographic evolution,
- political change,
- technological disruption.

Historical cases inconsistent with theoretical expectations should receive particular attention.

Repeated historical contradictions warrant framework revision.

---

# 7. Comparative Evaluation

The framework should not be evaluated in isolation.

Instead, competing explanations should be compared according to:

- explanatory scope,
- theoretical coherence,
- empirical consistency,
- predictive capability,
- methodological transparency.

Scientific progress is achieved by comparing alternative explanations rather than seeking absolute confirmation.

---

# 8. Scientific Humility

The framework should never be considered permanently validated.

Future evidence may:

- improve existing mechanisms,
- refine formalization,
- identify new variables,
- revise empirical interpretation,
- challenge existing assumptions.

Accordingly, falsification should be viewed as an opportunity for scientific advancement rather than failure.

---

# 9. Relationship to Repository Development

Repository development should follow the same philosophy.

New documents should:

- preserve validated knowledge,
- revise unsupported assumptions,
- document changes transparently,
- maintain reproducibility,
- encourage independent evaluation.

The repository itself therefore evolves according to the same adaptive principles proposed for social systems.

---

# 10. Conclusion

The Social Systems Framework defines explicit conditions under which its propositions and architecture may be challenged.

By distinguishing:

- measurement failure,
- specification failure,
- missing mechanisms,
- model failure,

the framework promotes disciplined scientific evaluation while avoiding both premature rejection and unfalsifiable flexibility.

The objective is not to demonstrate that the framework cannot be falsified.

Rather, the objective is to ensure that when disagreement occurs, researchers can identify **why** it occurs and respond proportionally.

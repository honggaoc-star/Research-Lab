# 01. Evidence Taxonomy

## Purpose

The Social Systems Framework seeks to explain how social systems evolve through interactions among institutions, human behavior, resource allocation, and adaptive dynamics. Evaluating such a framework requires multiple forms of empirical evidence.

This document establishes a taxonomy of evidence used throughout the repository. Rather than treating evidence as a single homogeneous category, the framework distinguishes different forms of observation according to their role in scientific evaluation.

The objective is to organize empirical evidence systematically, clarify its relationship to theory, and provide a common vocabulary for subsequent empirical research.

---

# 1. Why an Evidence Taxonomy?

Scientific theories are rarely evaluated using a single dataset or research method.

Instead, confidence develops through the accumulation of multiple complementary forms of evidence.

Different evidence serves different purposes.

Some observations measure state variables.

Others evaluate causal mechanisms.

Others test long-term predictions.

Still others reveal limitations or motivate refinement.

Recognizing these distinctions improves both scientific clarity and empirical rigor.

# 2. Categories of Evidence

The framework recognizes six primary categories of evidence.

## 2.1 Operational Evidence

Evidence that establishes how theoretical constructs become measurable.

Examples include:

- state-variable definitions,
- operationalization,
- observable indicators,
- measurement protocols.

## 2.4 Causal Evidence

Examples include:

- longitudinal analysis,
- comparative studies,
- natural experiments,
- simulation,
- counterfactual analysis.

Examples include:

- technological transitions,
- demographic change,

# 3. Relationships Among Evidence Types

These categories are complementary rather than independent.

Operational evidence defines what can be measured.

Observational evidence supplies the measurements.

Measurement evidence evaluates their reliability.

Causal evidence examines theoretical mechanisms.

Historical evidence evaluates long-run system evolution.

Developmental evidence identifies future research priorities.

Together they establish the empirical foundation of the Social Systems Framework.

# 5. Principles

All empirical evidence should satisfy several general principles.

## Transparency

Methods, assumptions, and limitations should be documented explicitly.

---

## Reproducibility

Independent researchers should be able to reproduce empirical findings using documented procedures.

---

## Comparability

Measurements should support meaningful comparison across time and institutional settings whenever possible.

---

## Multiple Independent Sources

Confidence increases when different forms of evidence converge upon similar conclusions.

No individual dataset or research method should determine the validity of the framework by itself.

---

## Falsifiability

Evidence should be capable of challenging as well as supporting theoretical propositions.

Contradictory observations should motivate refinement rather than reinterpretation.

---

# 6. Evidence and Scientific Progress

Scientific confidence develops cumulatively.

The framework therefore evaluates evidence according to:

- conceptual consistency,
- empirical quality,
- methodological rigor,
- reproducibility,
- convergence across independent sources.

This cumulative perspective recognizes that scientific understanding improves through progressive refinement rather than single decisive observations.

---

# 7. Relationship to Validation

The Evidence folder establishes how observations are obtained, interpreted, and evaluated.

The Validation folder examines whether those observations support the theoretical framework.

Evidence therefore provides the empirical foundation upon which scientific validation depends.

# Dynamic Social Systems

## A Dynamic Framework for the Evolution of Social Systems

This research program develops a general framework for understanding how social systems evolve through interactions among heterogeneous actors, institutional structures, decision rules, historical contingencies, and recursive feedback.

The program began as a project on social inequality. As the framework developed, inequality became one application of a broader theory of social allocation, development, institutional adaptation, and social-system evolution.

## Central Research Question

How do social systems transform initial differences, resources, institutions, and shocks into changing allocations of opportunities, capabilities, influence, risk, and well-being over time?

## Foundational Axioms

1. **Heterogeneity**  
   Individuals and organizations differ in endowments, capabilities, preferences, information, identities, resources, and circumstances.

2. **Institutional Translation**  
   Institutions determine how heterogeneous characteristics are translated into opportunities, constraints, responsibilities, and rewards.

3. **Contingency**  
   Social development is continually affected by luck, timing, shocks, technological change, and historical events.

4. **Recursive Feedback**  
   Observed outcomes reshape institutions, incentives, beliefs, organizations, resources, and opportunities, thereby influencing subsequent rounds of social evolution.

## Core Architecture

The framework distinguishes:

1. system boundary;
2. state variables;
3. primitive drivers;
4. decision rules;
5. evolutionary mechanisms;
6. observable outcomes;
7. recursive feedback.

## Development and Allocation

The framework treats social evolution as the joint movement of:

- **Development** — the level or expected value of social outcomes;
- **Allocation** — the distribution of those outcomes across individuals and groups.

Inequality is one observable property of the allocation dimension.

## Distributed Adaptive Systems

Social systems exhibit feedback, adaptation, delay, amplification, damping, and regime transition. Unlike engineered systems, however, societies usually have no single controller, common objective function, or centralized optimization process.

Social evolution is therefore modeled as an emergent outcome of distributed decision-making by households, firms, governments, organizations, networks, and other actors.

## Current Research Priorities

- define the minimum sufficient state vector;
- formalize the joint evolution of development and allocation;
- test whether the framework generates predictions and counterfactuals;
- identify falsification and revision criteria;
- compare the framework with established theories;
- develop historical and empirical applications.

# Dynamic Social Systems

## A General Framework for the Evolution of Social Systems

The Dynamic Social Systems research program develops a general framework for understanding how social systems evolve through interactions among heterogeneous actors, institutional structures, decision rules, historical contingencies, shocks, and recursive feedback.

The project began as an investigation of social inequality. As the framework developed, inequality became one substantive application of a broader theory of social development, allocation, institutional adaptation, and system evolution.

---

## Central Research Question

> How do social systems transform initial differences, resources, institutions, decisions, and shocks into changing distributions of opportunity, capability, influence, risk, and well-being over time?

The framework examines both:

* **development** — changes in the level or expected value of social outcomes; and
* **allocation** — changes in how those outcomes are distributed across individuals, groups, organizations, and regions.

Inequality is therefore treated as an important property of social allocation, but not as the entire object of analysis.

---

## Foundational Axioms

### 1. Heterogeneity

Individuals, households, organizations, and institutions differ in endowments, capabilities, preferences, information, identities, resources, constraints, and circumstances.

### 2. Institutional Translation

Institutions determine how heterogeneous characteristics are translated into opportunities, constraints, responsibilities, influence, risks, and rewards.

### 3. Contingency

Social development is affected by luck, timing, shocks, technological change, historical events, and other conditions that are not fully controlled by individual actors.

### 4. Recursive Feedback

Observed outcomes reshape institutions, incentives, beliefs, organizations, resources, political power, and future opportunities, thereby influencing subsequent rounds of social evolution.

---

## Core Architecture

The framework distinguishes among:

1. system boundaries;
2. state variables;
3. primitive drivers;
4. actor heterogeneity;
5. institutional structures;
6. decision rules;
7. transition mechanisms;
8. shocks and contingencies;
9. observable outcomes;
10. recursive feedback.

A general social system may be represented as an evolving state governed by endogenous transition processes, institutional constraints, adaptive behavior, and changing external conditions.

The framework does not assume that society has a single controller, common objective function, or centralized optimization process. Social evolution instead emerges from distributed decisions made by households, firms, governments, organizations, networks, communities, and other actors.

## Research Architecture

The repository separates the research program into distinct scientific functions.

```text
Research-Lab/
└── Social-Systems/
    ├── README.md
    ├── Framework/
    ├── Formalization/
    ├── Literature/
    ├── Validation/
    ├── Evidence/
    ├── Applications/
    ├── Working-Paper/
    ├── Figures/
    ├── Red-Team/
    └── Archive/
```

### `Framework/`

Defines the general conceptual architecture of the research program.

It contains the foundational assumptions, core principles, system architecture, and high-level explanation of how the major components relate.

The Framework is broader than any individual mathematical model or empirical application.

### `Formalization/`

Develops bounded mathematical and computational representations of selected mechanisms within the conceptual framework.

Current formalization tracks include:

* Minimum State Vector;
* Institutional Translation;
* Endogenous Transition Dynamics;
* Adaptive Intervention Dynamics.

Formal models are treated as limited representations of specific questions rather than complete models of society.

### `Literature/`

Reviews and organizes the bodies of scholarship relevant to the framework.

The literature program evaluates how the framework relates to established work in areas such as:

* Institutional Economics;
* Human Capital Theory;
* Political Economy;
* Behavioral Economics;
* Complexity Science;
* Distribution Dynamics;
* Evolutionary Economics;
* Welfare Economics;
* Social Mobility;
* Inequality Measurement.

### `Validation/`

Evaluates the scientific defensibility and readiness of the framework.

It contains:

* literature-validation synthesis;
* contribution assessment;
* research positioning;
* testable propositions;
* falsification criteria;
* historical-validation protocols;
* readiness assessment;
* validation summary.

Validation defines what must be tested and what evidence would support, narrow, revise, or reject the framework.

### `Evidence/`

Contains the historical, empirical, and data foundations used to test the framework.

Its principal subdivisions are:

```text
Evidence/
├── Historical/
├── Empirical/
└── Data/
```

Evidence is used to challenge the framework rather than merely illustrate it. It records actual historical studies, empirical analyses, data documentation, mechanism tests, robustness exercises, and contradictory findings.

### `Applications/`

Applies the general framework to substantive research domains.

The first major application is social inequality, including the evolution of income, wealth, opportunity, mobility, political influence, and exposure to risk.

Additional applications may be developed only where the common framework generates useful and testable insights.

### `Working-Paper/`

Develops the circulation-quality manuscript.

The working paper synthesizes the central theoretical contribution, selected formal results, relevant literature, evidence, applications, limitations, and implications.

It is a communication layer rather than the repository’s technical source of truth.

### `Figures/`

Stores conceptual diagrams, formal schematics, empirical figures, and publication-ready visual materials.

### `Red-Team/`

Contains adversarial reviews, unresolved objections, alternative explanations, failure tests, and critiques of major theoretical and empirical claims.

### `Archive/`

Preserves superseded drafts, earlier project structures, abandoned approaches, and historical records of the research program.

Archived materials are retained for traceability but should not be treated as current statements of the framework.

## Scientific Workflow

The research program follows an iterative rather than strictly linear process:

```text
Framework
    ↓
Formalization
    ↓
Validation
    ↓
Evidence
    ↓
Applications
    ↓
Working Paper
```

Results may also move backward through the sequence:

```text
Evidence
    ↓
Model revision
    ↓
Framework narrowing or clarification
    ↓
Further validation
```

The intended cycle is:

1. define the conceptual architecture;
2. formalize a bounded mechanism;
3. derive propositions and observable implications;
4. test those implications against historical and empirical evidence;
5. compare performance with simpler alternative explanations;
6. retain, narrow, revise, or reject the relevant claim;
7. communicate validated results through the working paper.

---

## Current Research Priorities

The immediate priorities are:

1. preserve the conceptual distinction between the general Framework and its bounded formal models;
2. complete the remaining approved formalization tracks;
3. translate formal mechanisms into testable propositions and measurable variables;
4. conduct historical and empirical testing under the Validation protocols;
5. document negative, null, and contradictory evidence;
6. complete the social-inequality application as the first end-to-end demonstration;
7. prepare a concise circulation-quality working paper.

The project should now emphasize depth, integration, and testing rather than continued expansion of the foundational architecture.

---

## Research Standards

All major theoretical and empirical work should meet the following standards:

* explicit system boundaries;
* clearly defined concepts and variables;
* distinction between assumptions, mechanisms, and outcomes;
* transparent links between formalization and evidence;
* comparison with simpler alternative explanations;
* explicit falsification and revision criteria;
* preservation of negative and null results;
* source provenance and reproducibility;
* separation of descriptive, causal, and normative claims;
* acknowledgment of uncertainty and scope limitations.

The framework should not receive explanatory credit for patterns already fully accounted for by a simpler theory.

---

## Current Status

The conceptual framework and validation architecture are substantially developed.

Formalization is active, with major progress completed in Institutional Translation and Endogenous Transition Dynamics. The Evidence phase is now the principal bridge between the theoretical architecture and the first full application.

The framework remains provisional. Its concepts, models, and scope may be narrowed or revised as they are tested against historical evidence, empirical data, competing theories, and adversarial review.

The long-term objective is not to construct a universal model of society. It is to develop a disciplined and extensible research framework for studying how institutions, heterogeneity, contingency, adaptation, and recursive feedback jointly shape social-system evolution.

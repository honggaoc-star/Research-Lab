# Core Assumptions

## Purpose

This document makes explicit the principal assumptions underlying the new conceptual architecture.

They are working assumptions, not established laws. Each may be refined, bounded, or rejected when formalization, measurement, or empirical comparison reveals problems.

---

## 1. Analytical Systems Can Be Bounded

Research can identify a focal system and distinguish it provisionally from an environment.

The boundary is question-dependent rather than absolute. It must be stated, justified, and revised when it hides relevant internal variation or feedback.

---

## 2. Systems and Environments Are Dynamically Interdependent

Systems are affected by environments through interaction, but they may also modify environments.

Institutions, technologies, markets, information, and opportunity landscapes may therefore be endogenous to repeated interaction.

One-way environmental explanations are insufficient when the focal system materially changes later conditions.

---

## 3. Uncertainty Is Irreducible for Practical Analysis

Relevant states, events, interactions, and consequences cannot always be fully determined, known, predicted, or controlled from the ex ante position.

This assumption does not require a metaphysical claim that all uncertainty is fundamental. Epistemic, model-based, strategic, dynamic, and stochastic uncertainty can be scientifically consequential even when their ultimate status differs.

---

## 4. Luck Is Not a Primitive

Luck is an evaluative and explanatory concept applied to consequential variation within relationships among systems, environments, interactions, and uncertainty.

It should not be used as a causal force or an unexplained residual.

A claim about luck must identify the affected system, relevant uncertainty, consequential interaction, limits of prior determination, and changed state or trajectory.

---

## 5. Opportunity and Constraint Are Relational

Opportunities and constraints do not exist independently of systems.

They depend on system position, environmental conditions, access, capability, objectives, institutions, timing, and possible interaction.

The same condition may have different meanings for different systems or at different horizons.

---

## 6. Capability and Luck Interact

Capability affects perception, search, learning, coordination, response, adaptation, and environmental influence.

Luck affects exposure, timing, access, information, constraints, and the returns to capability.

Observed success normally reflects interaction rather than a clean division between “skill” and “luck.” Neither should be inferred solely from outcomes.

---

## 7. Realized Outcomes Do Not Exhaust Relevant Possibilities

The realized trajectory is one path among alternatives that were feasible or conceivable ex ante.

Unrealized and foreclosed opportunities matter, although evidence about them is often weaker.

Counterfactual analysis should remain disciplined by the system’s prior state, information, constraints, and feasible interactions.

---

## 8. State and Trajectory Must Be Distinguished

A state describes a system at a time. A trajectory is a sequence of states and transitions.

Immediate outcomes may differ from long-term effects. Feedback can amplify, attenuate, reverse, stabilize, or transform an initial difference.

Luck should therefore be evaluated over explicit horizons.

---

## 9. Interaction Is the Location of Mechanism

Mechanisms specify how systems and environments affect one another.

Exposure, recognition, exchange, conflict, cooperation, experimentation, learning, selection, feedback, and institutionalization are candidate interaction processes.

Naming a source or outcome is not equivalent to specifying a mechanism.

---

## 10. Adaptation Is Possible but Not Automatically Beneficial

Systems may alter behavior, structure, capabilities, or relationships in response to conditions.

Adaptation can improve fit, reduce vulnerability, exploit opportunity, create lock-in, shift burdens, or become maladaptive after environmental change.

Survival is not sufficient proof of superior adaptation.

---

## 11. Selection Does Not Establish Superiority

Differential persistence, growth, or visibility may reflect capability, but also timing, inherited position, power, exposure, institutional support, environmental fit, and feedback.

Survivors are selected observations. Explanations should seek failed, suppressed, delayed, and unrealized alternatives where evidence permits.

---

## 12. Levels of Analysis Matter

Individual, organizational, institutional, ecosystem, societal, and civilizational processes should not be collapsed.

A variable may be internal at one level and environmental at another. Benefits and harms may be distributed differently across components and levels.

Cross-level claims require explicit connecting mechanisms.

---

## 13. Evaluation Is Perspective- and Horizon-Dependent

“Good luck” and “bad luck” require an evaluative standard, affected system, and time horizon.

A development may be favorable to one system and adverse to another, or favorable initially and harmful later.

Descriptive mechanism claims should be separated from normative judgments.

---

## 14. Institutions Mediate Consequential Variation

Institutions distribute exposure, information, resources, authority, opportunity, and protection.

They may amplify advantage, buffer harm, socialize risk, concentrate opportunity, or alter future uncertainty.

Institutions are not currently separate primitives; they may be systems, system structures, or environmental structures depending on the question.

---

## 15. The Architecture Must Remain Revisable

System, Environment, Interaction, and Uncertainty are working primitives.

The set should be revised if it repeatedly:

- distorts essential phenomena;
- collapses distinct causal roles;
- prevents meaningful measurement;
- creates unresolved cross-level ambiguity;
- or fails to improve comparison.

Importance alone does not justify adding a primitive. Irreducibility within the research program is the relevant test.

---

## 16. Methodological Commitments

The project proceeds through a recursive sequence:

1. define concepts;
2. specify relationships;
3. formalize mechanisms;
4. operationalize constructs;
5. test empirical cases;
6. compare across domains;
7. promote robust relationships cautiously;
8. revise the architecture when evidence requires.

No stage is final. Empirical evidence may revise foundations, and measurement problems may expose theoretical ambiguity.

---

## 17. Falsification and Revision Questions

The architecture should be challenged by asking:

- Can cases be described coherently without one of the proposed primitives?
- Does a supposedly derived concept perform an irreducible role?
- Do boundary choices systematically determine conclusions?
- Can opportunity and constraint be operationalized relationally?
- Are ex ante uncertainty and ex post attribution empirically distinguishable?
- Which mechanisms recur across domains, and where do they fail?
- Do alternative explanations account for apparent luck more precisely?
- What evidence would force revision of the primitive set?

---

## Conclusion

These assumptions provide a transparent starting point for the Research on Luck program.

Their purpose is not to protect the architecture from criticism, but to make its commitments visible enough to formalize, measure, test, compare, and revise.

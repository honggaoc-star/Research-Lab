# Opportunity and Constraints

## Purpose

This document develops **Opportunity** and **Constraint** as derived relational concepts.

Neither is a working primitive. Both arise from the relationship among a system, its environment, possible interactions, and uncertainty.

---

## 1. Opportunity

> **An opportunity is a system–environment relationship in which a possible interaction could expand or improve the system’s accessible trajectories relative to a specified objective or evaluative standard.**

An opportunity is not simply an external object. Its existence and significance depend on:

- environmental conditions;
- the focal system’s position and capabilities;
- access to relevant interaction;
- timing;
- information and recognition;
- resources;
- institutions;
- and the objective or standard used for evaluation.

The same condition may be an opportunity for one system, a threat to another, and irrelevant to a third.

---

## 2. Constraint

> **A constraint is a system–environment relationship that restricts feasible interactions or accessible trajectories.**

Constraints may be:

- physical;
- informational;
- resource-based;
- technological;
- institutional;
- social;
- political;
- temporal;
- or capability-based.

A constraint is not necessarily permanent or wholly external. Systems may modify constraints through learning, coordination, innovation, institutional change, or environmental transformation.

Some constraints protect systems from harmful exposure. Removing a constraint can therefore create both opportunity and vulnerability.

---

## 3. Opportunity Landscape

The **opportunity landscape** is the structured set of opportunities and constraints relevant to a focal system at a specified time.

It includes:

- recognized and unrecognized possibilities;
- accessible and inaccessible possibilities;
- competing paths;
- costs and complementarities;
- institutional permissions;
- timing windows;
- and uncertainty about consequences.

The landscape is dynamic. It changes as systems learn, other systems act, resources move, institutions evolve, and technologies create new interactions.

“Landscape” is an analytical metaphor, not a claim that opportunities exist independently of systems.

---

## 4. Potential, Access, Recognition, and Realization

These stages should be distinguished.

### Potential opportunity

A system–environment relation could support a trajectory change.

### Accessible opportunity

The system can feasibly enter the relevant interaction.

### Recognized opportunity

The system interprets the possibility as significant.

### Pursued opportunity

The system commits action or resources.

### Realized opportunity

Interaction produces an observed trajectory change.

### Partially realized opportunity

Only part of the possible change occurs.

An opportunity may fail at any transition. Failure to realize it does not show that it never existed, and recognition does not guarantee feasibility.

---

## 5. Unrealized and Foreclosed Opportunities

An **unrealized opportunity** is a plausible possibility that was not converted into the relevant trajectory change.

It may be:

- unrecognized;
- inaccessible;
- rejected;
- mistimed;
- resource-constrained;
- institutionally blocked;
- unsuccessfully pursued;
- or displaced by another objective.

A **foreclosed opportunity** is a path made inaccessible by prior states or interactions.

These categories are important because observed outcomes contain only realized paths. Research limited to survivors and successes systematically understates alternatives.

Counterfactual claims should remain disciplined by ex ante feasibility and available evidence.

---

## 6. Opportunity Creation and Migration

Systems do not merely discover fixed opportunities. They may create them by:

- generating knowledge;
- building technology;
- reorganizing resources;
- establishing institutions;
- coordinating other systems;
- or changing environmental conditions.

Opportunities may also migrate. A possibility first available to one system may move to another through diffusion, imitation, competition, regulation, or delay.

This makes opportunity endogenous to interaction.

---

## 7. Constraint Transformation

Constraints can be:

- relaxed;
- bypassed;
- redistributed;
- internalized;
- amplified;
- or transformed into enabling structures.

Institutions may pool risk while limiting choice. Standards may constrain variation while enabling coordination. Scarcity may stimulate search but also exclude systems lacking resources.

The analysis should avoid treating “more opportunity” and “fewer constraints” as automatically equivalent to improvement.

---

## 8. Opportunity Under Uncertainty

Systems may be uncertain about:

- whether a possibility exists;
- its probability of success;
- its required capabilities;
- its duration;
- other systems’ responses;
- and its long-term consequences.

Search, experimentation, and learning may reveal opportunities, but they consume resources and can alter the landscape.

This relationship links [12-Uncertainty.md](12-Uncertainty.md) to adaptive capacity and dynamic processes.

---

## 9. Luck Potential, Realization, and Attribution

The opportunity framework supports a three-part sequence:

### Ex ante: Luck Potential

The system occupies an opportunity landscape containing uncertain possible interactions and constraints.

### Transition: Luck Realization

Exposure, recognition, response, and feedback convert some possibilities into realized trajectory changes while others remain unrealized or are foreclosed.

### Ex post: Luck Attribution

Observers interpret the realized path as good luck, bad luck, missed opportunity, avoided harm, or unexpected discovery.

Luck is not the opportunity itself. It is attributed to consequential variation across this sequence when the realized change was not fully determined by prior intention, capability, or action.

---

## 10. Comparative Questions

Cases should ask:

1. Which opportunities were present?
2. Which systems had access?
3. What constraints differed?
4. What was recognized ex ante?
5. Which capabilities affected response?
6. What timing window applied?
7. Which opportunity was realized?
8. Which plausible alternatives remained unrealized?
9. What feedback changed the later landscape?
10. Favorable or adverse for whom, and over what horizon?

---

## 11. Relationship to Later Work

[Formalization](../Formalization/README.md) should represent opportunity as a relational state or accessible transition rather than an unexplained external input.

[Measurement](../Measurement/README.md) should distinguish potential, access, recognition, pursuit, and realization.

Empirical studies should examine both realized and unrealized cases where evidence permits. Comparative work should identify which mechanisms broaden, concentrate, migrate, or foreclose opportunity.

---

## Conclusion

Opportunity and constraint are dynamic relationships between systems and environments.

They structure possible interactions but do not determine outcomes. Their consequences depend on uncertainty, access, recognition, capability, response, feedback, and time. This relational account makes unrealized alternatives part of the research program rather than invisible residue outside the realized path.

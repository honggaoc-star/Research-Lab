# Case Studies

## Cross-Domain Empirical Evaluation of the Research on Luck Framework

---

# Overview

The **Case Studies** directory evaluates the conceptual, theoretical, and empirical framework developed throughout the **Research on Luck** project through systematic analysis of real-world events, individuals, organizations, institutions, and historical processes.

While **Measurement** establishes how luck may be studied scientifically, **Case Studies** examines whether the proposed framework successfully explains observed trajectories across multiple domains of human activity.

Rather than serving merely as illustrations, case studies function as independent empirical evaluations of the broader theoretical framework.

Each domain asks the same scientific question:

> **Does the proposed theory explain observed trajectories more effectively than competing explanations?**

---

# Objectives

The primary objectives of this directory are to:

* Evaluate the explanatory power of the Research on Luck framework across diverse domains.
* Compare competing explanations for observed outcomes.
* Examine how stochastic events alter opportunity spaces.
* Analyze the interaction among luck, skill, institutions, networks, and historical context.
* Identify recurring mechanisms operating across different fields.
* Refine the theoretical framework through empirical observation.
* Generate new hypotheses for future theoretical and empirical research.

---

# Scientific Role

Case studies occupy a unique position within the overall research program.

Unlike the preceding sections, which develop concepts, theory, and methodology, this directory examines whether those ideas successfully explain real-world phenomena.

Accordingly, case studies perform three complementary scientific functions.

First,

they evaluate the explanatory power of the proposed framework.

Second,

they identify limitations requiring theoretical refinement.

Third,

they generate new hypotheses that may guide future conceptual, theoretical, and empirical research.

The objective is not to confirm the theory, but to challenge and improve it.

# Why Multiple Domains?

A theory explaining only scientific discovery remains a theory of scientific discovery.

A theory explaining:

* science,
* technology,
* business,
* finance,
* organizations,
* public policy,
* and history

has the potential to become a general theory of luck.

Studying multiple domains therefore provides stronger empirical tests than repeated investigation within a single field.

Agreement across domains strengthens confidence in the generality of the framework.

Systematic differences identify opportunities for theoretical refinement.

---

# Analytical Framework

Every case study should follow a common analytical structure.

1. Background
2. Initial Conditions
3. The Stochastic Event(s)
4. Opportunity Space Before the Event
5. Opportunity Space After the Event
6. Path Dependence
7. Feedback and Amplification
8. Network Effects
9. Institutional Mediation
10. Counterfactual Analysis
11. Alternative Explanations
12. Evaluation of the Framework
13. Lessons Learned

Although different domains emphasize different mechanisms, maintaining a common analytical structure facilitates systematic comparison.

---

# Guiding Principles

Case studies should:

* distinguish observed facts from theoretical interpretation;
* compare multiple competing explanations;
* avoid attributing every outcome to luck;
* explicitly identify the roles of preparation, skill, institutions, and historical context;
* recognize uncertainty in causal interpretation;
* use evidence to evaluate theory rather than merely illustrate it;
* remain open to findings that challenge the framework.

Scientific progress depends upon critical evaluation rather than confirmation alone.

---

# Comparative Analysis

While each case is analyzed independently, the long-term objective is comparison across domains.

Examples include:

* scientific discovery versus technological innovation;
* entrepreneurship versus finance;
* organizations versus public policy;
* historical change versus individual achievement.

Comparative analysis seeks to identify recurring mechanisms governing:

* opportunity creation,
* opportunity recognition,
* opportunity development,
* institutional amplification,
* network propagation,
* and long-term trajectory evolution.

These comparisons provide stronger evidence than isolated case studies.

# Relationship to Applications

Case Studies evaluate the theory.

Applications extend validated knowledge into practice.

Accordingly,

case studies should establish explanatory credibility before practical recommendations are developed.

Applications therefore represent the next stage of the research program rather than a substitute for empirical evaluation.

---

# Long-Term Goal

The long-term goal of the **Case Studies** directory is to develop a cumulative body of empirical analyses capable of evaluating, challenging, and refining the broader theoretical framework proposed throughout the Research on Luck project.

Rather than treating luck as an isolated event or a retrospective label, the case studies seek to explain how stochastic events interact with opportunity spaces, dynamic trajectories, institutions, and networks across diverse domains of human activity.

Ultimately, this directory aims to determine whether a common set of underlying mechanisms can explain the evolution of opportunity across science, technology, business, finance, organizations, public policy, history, and other domains. If such mechanisms consistently emerge, they provide strong evidence that the proposed framework extends beyond individual examples toward a genuinely general theory of luck.

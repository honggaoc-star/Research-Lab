# Measurement Architecture

## Purpose

This document defines the additive measurement architecture for the **Research on Luck** project.

The preserved Measurement files 01–07 established the first-generation empirical framework. The new series operationalizes the later Foundations and Formalization architecture without rewriting the original documents.

Measurement does not mean assigning one number to luck. It means connecting specified concepts and relationships to observable evidence while keeping inference, uncertainty, and alternative explanations explicit.

---

## 1. Objects of Measurement

The architecture distinguishes:

- **construct** — the theoretical concept of interest;
- **indicator** — observable evidence associated with a construct;
- **measure** — a rule for recording or combining indicators;
- **state** — a configuration at a specified time;
- **event** — a temporally located change;
- **relationship** — a hypothesized connection;
- **mechanism** — the process through which change occurs;
- **outcome** — an evaluated state or change at a chosen horizon.

An indicator is not the construct itself. Several indicators may measure one construct, and one indicator may reflect several constructs.

---

## 2. Measurement Layers

### Layer 0 — Working primitives

Operationalize system boundary and level, environmental scope, interaction or exposure, and uncertainty and information position.

### Layer 1 — Derived conditions

Operationalize opportunity landscape, constraints, information, resources, and variation.

### Layer 2 — System capabilities

Operationalize perception, search, learning, coordination, and adaptive capacity.

### Layer 3 — Dynamic processes

Operationalize response and non-response, selection, adaptation, discovery, and innovation.

### Layer 4 — Long-term effects

Operationalize amplification and attenuation, path dependence, resilience, transformation, and regime change.

### Layer 5 — Outcome evaluation

Operationalize evidence supporting realized opportunity, favorable or adverse trajectory change, missed or foreclosed opportunity, avoided harm, and unexpected discovery.

Outcome evaluation must specify the focal system, standard, and horizon.

---

## 3. Concepts and Relationships

The new Formalization series treats concepts as nodes and relationships as hypotheses.

Measurement must evaluate both whether the nodes are represented adequately and whether evidence supports the proposed relationship.

For a proposed relationship from construct A to construct B, an empirical design should specify:

- indicators for A and B;
- temporal order;
- mechanism evidence;
- boundary conditions;
- plausible confounders;
- competing relationships;
- and observations that would challenge the claim.

A correlation between indicators does not establish the underlying relationship.

---

## 4. Levels of Observation

Evidence may be gathered at individual, team, organization, institution, network, ecosystem, society, political-order, and cross-system levels.

The level of the indicator should match the level of the construct or be connected through an explicit aggregation or cross-level model.

Aggregate resilience, for example, should not be inferred from selected individual survivors.

---

## 5. Time and Sequence

Measurement should identify:

- baseline state;
- exposure time;
- recognition time;
- response time;
- intermediate states;
- feedback delays;
- and evaluation horizon.

Cross-sectional data may describe associations but often cannot identify trajectory mechanisms.

When historical data are incomplete, sequence claims should be graded by evidentiary strength rather than filled with retrospective narrative.

---

## 6. Direct, Indirect, and Latent Evidence

### Direct observation

The construct or event is recorded with limited inference, such as a documented policy change or transaction.

### Indirect observation

Indicators provide evidence about the construct, such as search behavior indicating attention.

### Latent construct

The concept requires multiple indicators or a model, such as adaptive capacity.

### Mechanism evidence

Records show how change occurred, such as contemporaneous correspondence, process logs, institutional decisions, or sequence-specific contrasts.

Measurement plans should state which category applies.

---

## 7. Multiple Methods

Appropriate methods may include structured historical comparison, longitudinal data, process tracing, natural or randomized experiments, network analysis, event-history analysis, sequence analysis, surveys, interviews, archival reconstruction, computational simulation, and mixed methods.

No method is superior independent of the question. Method selection should follow the construct, relationship, level, and available evidence.

---

## 8. Measurement Uncertainty

Every measurement should distinguish:

- uncertainty in the underlying process;
- uncertainty in the system’s ex ante information;
- measurement error;
- missing data;
- model uncertainty;
- classification uncertainty;
- and counterfactual uncertainty.

These should not be collapsed into a single error term and labeled luck.

Where possible, report ranges, alternative classifications, sensitivity, or confidence categories.

---

## 9. Measurement Record Template

For each construct or relationship, record:

1. name;
2. conceptual definition;
3. formal role;
4. focal system;
5. level and boundary;
6. time and horizon;
7. observable indicators;
8. data source;
9. coding or calculation rule;
10. missingness;
11. reliability;
12. construct validity;
13. alternative interpretations;
14. uncertainty;
15. relationship tested;
16. evidence that would challenge the inference.

This template supports comparison without forcing identical indicators across domains.

---

## 10. Cross-Domain Comparability

Science, Technology, Business, History, and Politics may require different indicators for the same construct.

Comparability should be based on a common conceptual definition, comparable formal role, explicit domain-specific operationalization, and documented differences.

Identical variables are not required. Unexamined conceptual drift is not acceptable.

---

## 11. Luck as an Inference

Luck is not directly observed as one quantity.

A defensible inference requires evidence about:

- ex ante uncertainty;
- system–environment interaction;
- exposure;
- limits of prior intention, capability, and control;
- consequential trajectory change;
- and feasible alternatives.

The strength of inference should be reported rather than asserted categorically.

---

## 12. Evidence Status

Measurement outputs should distinguish:

- descriptive observation;
- indicator construction;
- relationship association;
- mechanism support;
- causal identification;
- comparative recurrence;
- and unresolved ambiguity.

Formal precision does not raise the evidence status by itself.

---

## Conclusion

The measurement architecture connects conceptual nodes and formal relationships to evidence.

It favors disciplined inference over false precision, multiple complementary methods over one universal metric, and explicit uncertainty over residual labeling. Its purpose is to make the project’s relationship claims empirically vulnerable and therefore scientifically improvable.

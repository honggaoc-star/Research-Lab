# Mathematical Representation

## Representing Luck in Formal Models

---

# Abstract

The concept of luck is frequently discussed in philosophy, economics, psychology, and everyday language, yet it is rarely represented using a unified mathematical framework. Existing treatments often regard luck as an unexplained residual, an external shock, or an informal description of uncertainty.

This document develops a mathematical representation of luck suitable for interdisciplinary research. Rather than defining luck as a single variable, the framework represents luck as a multidimensional phenomenon that interacts with individual characteristics, environments, institutions, and time.

The objective is to establish a common mathematical language upon which subsequent formal models can be constructed.

---

# 1. Why Mathematical Representation?

Mathematical representation serves three purposes.

First, it forces conceptual precision. Ambiguous language is replaced with explicitly defined variables, assumptions, and relationships.

Second, it allows competing theories of luck to be compared objectively.

Third, it provides a foundation for simulation, empirical testing, and quantitative analysis.

The goal is not to reduce luck to a single equation, but to identify the components that any rigorous theory should represent.

---

# 2. Fundamental Components

Any realization of luck involves at least five conceptual components.

* an individual or entity experiencing the event;
* an environment in which uncertainty exists;
* one or more uncertain events;
* resulting outcomes;
* the passage of time.

These components provide the minimal structure required for formal analysis.

---

# 3. Basic Representation

Let

* **I** denote an individual, organization, or other decision-making entity;
* **E** denote the surrounding environment;
* **S** denote the state of the system;
* **X** denote uncertain events;
* **O** denote observable outcomes;
* **t** denote time.

Luck is not identified with any single one of these quantities.

Instead, luck emerges through their interaction.

Conceptually,

> **Luck = f(I, E, S, X, O, t)**

where **f(·)** represents an unknown relationship to be developed in later models.

This expression should be interpreted as a conceptual representation rather than a completed mathematical equation.

---

# 4. Luck as a Multidimensional Vector

The taxonomy developed in **Foundations** demonstrates that luck cannot be adequately described by a single scalar quantity.

Instead, each instance of luck may be represented by a vector of characteristics.

Possible dimensions include:

* origin,
* controllability,
* temporal structure,
* consequences,
* perception,
* valence,
* dynamics,
* observability.

Different research questions may emphasize different subsets of these dimensions.

This representation preserves the multidimensional nature of luck while remaining compatible with formal mathematical analysis.

---

# 5. Random Events versus Lucky Outcomes

A central distinction emerging from the interdisciplinary literature is that random events and lucky outcomes are not identical.

Random events describe uncertainty.

Lucky outcomes describe evaluations of those events relative to objectives, alternatives, and observers.

Accordingly,

not every random event is lucky,

and not every fortunate outcome results purely from randomness.

Formal models developed later should therefore distinguish between:

* stochastic events,
* resulting system states,
* and evaluations of those states.

---

# 6. Luck and State Transitions

Every uncertain event changes the state of a system.

Conceptually,

Current State

↓

Uncertain Event

↓

New State

Luck is associated with how uncertainty influences these state transitions.

Some transitions have negligible long-term consequences.

Others fundamentally alter future trajectories.

The mathematical representation should therefore emphasize changes in state rather than isolated events.

---

# 7. Dynamics

Luck unfolds through time.

An event occurring at one moment may influence opportunities many years later.

Accordingly,

formal models should distinguish:

* immediate effects,
* delayed effects,
* cumulative effects,
* path-dependent effects,
* and feedback effects.

These temporal properties become central subjects of later documents.

# 9. Modeling Assumptions

Unless otherwise specified,

models developed in this directory should assume:

* variables are explicitly defined;
* assumptions are transparent;
* uncertainty is represented explicitly;
* causal relationships are distinguished from statistical associations;
* domains of applicability are clearly stated.

These assumptions promote comparability among different models.

---

# 10. Limitations

No mathematical representation fully captures every aspect of luck.

Certain dimensions—including subjective experience, cultural beliefs, moral evaluation, and metaphysical interpretations—may resist complete formalization.

The purpose of mathematical representation is therefore not to replace conceptual analysis but to complement it.

Formal models should remain faithful to the conceptual foundations established elsewhere in the project.

---

# 12. Conclusion

A rigorous theory of luck requires more than probability alone. Luck emerges from the interaction of uncertain events, system states, individual characteristics, environments, institutions, and temporal dynamics.

Rather than reducing luck to a single mathematical quantity, this framework represents it as a structured, multidimensional phenomenon that can be examined at varying levels of formal precision. This approach provides a flexible foundation for subsequent theoretical development while remaining compatible with empirical investigation and interdisciplinary research.

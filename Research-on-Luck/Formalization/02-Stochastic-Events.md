# Stochastic Events

## Uncertainty as the Foundation of Luck

---

# Abstract

Luck cannot exist without uncertainty. Whether viewed through philosophy, economics, statistics, or psychology, fortunate and unfortunate outcomes arise because multiple future states are possible before an event occurs.

This document introduces stochastic events as the formal representation of uncertainty within the Research on Luck framework. Rather than equating luck with randomness itself, stochastic events provide the mechanism through which luck becomes possible.

The objective is to establish a common probabilistic language for subsequent models of dynamics, path dependence, institutional amplification, and cumulative advantage.

---

# 1. Introduction

The previous document introduced the components that a formal theory of luck must represent.

This document addresses the first mechanism underlying those components: uncertainty.

A deterministic universe leaves no room for chance outcomes. Conversely, a world containing uncertain events creates the possibility that otherwise similar individuals, organizations, or societies experience different trajectories.

Accordingly, stochastic events are not identical to luck, but they are a necessary condition for its existence.

---

# 2. Deterministic and Stochastic Systems

A useful distinction can be made between deterministic and stochastic systems.

In deterministic systems,

the current state uniquely determines future states.

Given identical initial conditions,

future outcomes are identical.

In stochastic systems,

multiple future outcomes remain possible.

The future is therefore represented not by a single trajectory but by a probability distribution over possible states.

Luck emerges only within this latter setting.

---

# 3. Stochastic Events

Let

**X**

denote a stochastic event.

Before the event occurs,

multiple outcomes remain possible.

After realization,

one outcome becomes observed.

Conceptually,

**X** transforms uncertainty into realized history.

The realization of **X** is random with respect to the available information before the event, although subsequent explanations may partially account for the observed outcome.

---

# 4. Randomness versus Luck

Randomness and luck are closely related but fundamentally different concepts.

Randomness describes uncertainty in the occurrence of events.

Luck describes the significance of realized outcomes.

A random event may have little practical consequence.

Conversely,

a single random event may permanently alter future opportunities.

Therefore,

randomness generates possibilities,

while luck concerns the realized consequences of those possibilities.

---

# 5. Probability Space

Formally,

uncertain events may be represented within a probability space consisting of:

* a collection of possible outcomes,
* a set of observable events,
* and a probability measure assigning likelihoods.

This representation provides a mathematically rigorous description of uncertainty without requiring any interpretation regarding whether an outcome is fortunate or unfortunate.

Probability therefore describes the structure of uncertainty,

not the existence of luck itself.

---

# 6. Realization and Outcome

The distinction between possibility and realization is central.

Before realization,

many futures exist.

After realization,

only one observed history remains.

Luck is evaluated after realization by comparing the observed outcome with the alternative outcomes that could reasonably have occurred.

This counterfactual comparison links probability theory with philosophical accounts of luck.

# 8. Independence and Dependence

Not all stochastic events are independent.

Some events occur independently.

Others exhibit dependence through shared causes, feedback, or environmental conditions.

This distinction is critical because many intuitive beliefs about luck incorrectly assume either complete independence or complete dependence.

Future documents examine these interactions through path dependence and dynamic systems.

---

# 9. From Events to Trajectories

An isolated stochastic event rarely represents the entire story.

Instead,

individuals experience sequences of uncertain events over time.

Some events have negligible consequences.

Others alter future opportunities, resources, and decisions.

The accumulation of realized events produces trajectories rather than isolated outcomes.

This observation motivates the subsequent study of the dynamics of luck.

---

# 10. Common Misconceptions

The interdisciplinary literature identifies several recurring misconceptions.

### Randomness implies equal outcomes.

False.

Random processes often produce highly unequal realizations.

---

### Independent events compensate each other.

Generally false.

Past realizations do not alter the probabilities of future independent events.

---

### Lucky individuals necessarily possess more luck.

Not necessarily.

Observed success may result from accumulated consequences, institutional amplification, or selection effects rather than persistent changes in underlying randomness.

---

### Every unlikely event indicates luck.

Not necessarily.

Unlikely events occur naturally within probabilistic systems.

Luck depends upon how those events relate to objectives, alternatives, and consequences.

# 12. Looking Ahead

This document establishes uncertainty as the formal foundation upon which the remainder of the theory is built.

The next document shifts attention from individual stochastic events to their evolution through time.

The central question becomes:

**How do random events influence future opportunities, and under what conditions do their effects disappear, accumulate, or become amplified?**

Answering this question requires moving beyond isolated probabilities toward dynamic systems.

---

# 13. Conclusion

Stochastic events provide the mathematical representation of uncertainty, but they do not by themselves constitute luck.

Luck emerges when uncertain events are realized within environments containing goals, alternative possibilities, institutions, and temporal dynamics.

By distinguishing randomness from the significance of realized outcomes, this framework establishes the foundation for a unified formal theory in which stochastic events generate opportunities while dynamic systems determine how their consequences evolve over time.

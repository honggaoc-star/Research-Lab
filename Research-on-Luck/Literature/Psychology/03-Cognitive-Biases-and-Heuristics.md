# Cognitive Biases and Heuristics

## Abstract

Human judgment under uncertainty is systematically influenced by cognitive biases and heuristics. Rather than evaluating uncertain events through formal statistical reasoning, individuals frequently rely on mental shortcuts that simplify complex decisions. These cognitive processes are efficient and often adaptive, but they can also produce systematic deviations from objective probability and rational inference.

This review examines the psychological literature on cognitive biases and heuristics as they relate to perceptions of luck. It argues that many beliefs about luck arise not from objective properties of uncertain events but from predictable features of human cognition.

Understanding these mechanisms provides an essential behavioral foundation for the broader **Research on Luck** project.

---

# 1. Introduction

People regularly make judgments under conditions of uncertainty.

In many situations, complete information is unavailable, time is limited, and future outcomes cannot be predicted with certainty.

Rather than performing formal probabilistic analysis, individuals typically rely upon cognitive heuristics—efficient mental strategies that simplify complex reasoning.

Although these heuristics often produce useful judgments, they may also generate systematic biases that influence perceptions of luck, coincidence, causality, and uncertainty.

Psychology investigates these processes to understand how individuals interpret uncertain events.

---

# 2. Heuristics as Efficient Reasoning

Heuristics are simplified cognitive strategies that enable rapid decision making.

Examples include judging events based upon:

* familiarity,
* similarity,
* recent experience,
* emotional salience,
* intuitive expectations.

These strategies reduce cognitive effort and often perform remarkably well in everyday environments.

However, because heuristics prioritize efficiency over exhaustive analysis, they sometimes produce biased judgments.

---

# 3. Common Cognitive Biases

The psychological literature identifies several biases that strongly influence perceptions of luck.

## Availability Heuristic

Events that are vivid, memorable, or emotionally significant are perceived as more common than they actually are.

Consequently, unusual lucky or unlucky experiences often appear more frequent than objective evidence suggests.

---

## Representativeness Heuristic

Individuals frequently judge probability according to how closely an event resembles an expected pattern.

This tendency can encourage beliefs that certain outcomes are "due" or "unlikely" despite statistical independence.

---

## Confirmation Bias

People naturally seek evidence supporting existing beliefs while overlooking contradictory evidence.

Individuals who believe themselves to be lucky often remember confirming experiences more readily than disconfirming ones.

---

## Hindsight Bias

After uncertain events occur, individuals frequently perceive outcomes as having been more predictable than they actually were.

This bias alters retrospective judgments concerning both luck and decision making.

---

## Illusion of Patterns

Human cognition is highly sensitive to patterns.

As a consequence, people sometimes perceive meaningful relationships within genuinely random sequences.

This tendency contributes to many beliefs concerning luck, coincidence, and fate.

---

# 4. Luck and Cognitive Bias

These biases demonstrate that perceptions of luck are influenced by systematic cognitive processes rather than by objective probability alone.

People frequently:

* overestimate rare events,
* detect patterns within randomness,
* remember unusual coincidences,
* underestimate uncertainty,
* reinterpret past events after outcomes become known.

Consequently, beliefs about luck often emerge through normal cognitive functioning rather than irrational thinking alone.

---

# 5. Adaptive Functions

Although cognitive biases are commonly discussed as sources of error, many heuristics also serve adaptive purposes.

Rapid judgments frequently enable effective action despite incomplete information.

Beliefs concerning luck may also influence:

* confidence,
* motivation,
* persistence,
* resilience,
* willingness to pursue opportunities.

Psychological biases therefore represent trade-offs between computational efficiency and perfect accuracy.

---

# 6. Contributions

Research on cognitive biases contributes several important insights.

First,

human judgment systematically departs from formal statistical reasoning.

Second,

many perceptions of luck arise from predictable cognitive mechanisms.

Third,

beliefs about luck influence subsequent decisions and behavior.

Fourth,

biases should be understood as characteristics of human cognition rather than simply errors in reasoning.

These findings substantially deepen understanding of the psychological experience of uncertainty.

---

# 7. Limitations

Cognitive biases explain how individuals interpret uncertain events but do not determine whether those events are objectively fortunate or unfortunate.

Likewise, psychological mechanisms cannot replace conceptual analysis, empirical investigation, or statistical reasoning.

Understanding bias therefore complements rather than substitutes for other disciplinary perspectives.

---

# 8. Implications for the Research on Luck Project

The psychological literature demonstrates that perceptions of luck are shaped by systematic features of human cognition.

For the **Research on Luck** project, this implies an important distinction.

Objective uncertainty and subjective interpretation should be analyzed separately.

Statistical models describe uncertain events.

Psychological processes explain how individuals perceive and respond to those events.

Integrating both perspectives provides a richer understanding of luck than either discipline can offer independently.

---

# 9. Conclusion

Research on cognitive biases and heuristics demonstrates that beliefs about luck arise through identifiable psychological mechanisms rather than through objective properties of uncertain events alone.

Heuristics simplify reasoning under uncertainty while simultaneously shaping perceptions of coincidence, probability, causality, and fortune.

For the **Research on Luck** project, these findings reinforce the importance of integrating behavioral science with conceptual, empirical, and statistical perspectives to develop a comprehensive theory of luck.

# Overview

## Abstract

Psychology investigates how individuals perceive, interpret, evaluate, and respond to uncertain events. Unlike philosophy, which examines the conceptual nature of luck, economics, which studies its influence on observable outcomes, and statistics, which develops methods for reasoning under uncertainty, psychology focuses on the cognitive and behavioral processes through which people experience luck.

This review examines the role of psychology in the scientific study of luck. It argues that psychological research does not determine whether luck objectively exists. Instead, it explains how beliefs about luck emerge, how people attribute outcomes to luck, and how perceptions of uncertainty influence judgment, emotion, and decision making.

Understanding these psychological processes is essential for developing a comprehensive interdisciplinary theory of luck.

---

# 1. Introduction

Human beings rarely experience uncertain events as purely statistical phenomena.

Instead, they interpret events through perception, memory, emotion, expectation, and prior experience.

The same event may therefore be regarded by different individuals as:

* lucky,
* unlucky,
* deserved,
* accidental,
* inevitable,
* or meaningless.

Psychology investigates the cognitive processes underlying these interpretations.

Rather than asking whether an event objectively reflects luck, psychology seeks to understand why people perceive events as lucky and how those perceptions influence subsequent behavior.

---

# 2. The Psychological Perspective

The psychological perspective differs from those of the preceding disciplines.

Its central objectives include understanding:

* perception,
* cognition,
* judgment,
* attribution,
* emotion,
* decision making.

Psychology therefore investigates the human interpretation of uncertain events rather than the events themselves.

This perspective complements conceptual, empirical, and statistical approaches to luck.

---

# 3. Core Psychological Concepts

Several concepts are central to psychological research on luck.

## Perception

Individuals actively interpret uncertain events rather than simply observing them.

Perception influences whether events are regarded as fortunate, unfortunate, or insignificant.

---

## Attribution

People naturally seek explanations for outcomes.

Successes and failures may be attributed to:

* skill,
* effort,
* circumstances,
* luck,
* or external influences.

These attributional processes strongly influence subsequent beliefs and behavior.

---

## Agency and Control

People differ in the extent to which they believe they can influence uncertain outcomes.

Perceived control frequently affects:

* confidence,
* persistence,
* risk taking,
* emotional responses.

These perceptions need not correspond to objective control.

---

## Cognitive Bias

Human judgment frequently departs from formal statistical reasoning.

Biases influence:

* probability judgments,
* causal inference,
* memory,
* expectations,
* decision making.

These systematic tendencies help explain why perceptions of luck often differ from objective uncertainty.

---

# 4. Psychology and Luck

Psychological research contributes important insights into how individuals experience luck.

Examples include:

* beliefs about being lucky,
* superstition,
* optimism,
* illusion of control,
* counterfactual thinking,
* attributional bias,
* decision making under uncertainty.

These phenomena demonstrate that experiences of luck depend not only upon external events but also upon human cognition.

---

# 5. Contributions

Psychology contributes several important perspectives.

First,

it explains why individuals interpret similar events differently.

Second,

it identifies systematic cognitive processes underlying beliefs about luck.

Third,

it demonstrates how perceptions of luck influence behavior.

Fourth,

it connects uncertainty with emotion, motivation, and decision making.

These contributions provide the behavioral foundation for understanding luck.

---

# 6. Limitations

Psychology primarily investigates subjective interpretation.

Consequently, psychological research does not determine:

* whether events are objectively lucky,
* how uncertainty should be represented mathematically,
* or how luck influences broader economic or institutional systems.

Instead, psychology explains how individuals perceive and respond to uncertain events.

These questions therefore complement rather than replace conceptual, empirical, and statistical perspectives.

---

# 7. Implications for the Research on Luck Project

The psychological literature demonstrates that luck is not experienced solely through external events.

Human cognition plays a central role in shaping how uncertain events are interpreted, remembered, and acted upon.

For the **Research on Luck** project, psychology therefore provides an essential behavioral perspective that complements philosophy, economics, and statistics.

Understanding luck requires understanding both uncertain events and the cognitive processes through which people assign meaning to those events.

---

# 8. An Emerging Perspective

A recurring theme throughout the literature is that objective uncertainty and subjective experience should be distinguished.

An uncertain event does not automatically become a psychologically meaningful experience.

Instead, individuals interpret uncertainty through prior beliefs, expectations, emotions, and social context.

This distinction suggests that luck should be understood as involving both external circumstances and internal interpretation.

---

# 9. Conclusion

Psychology provides the behavioral foundation for understanding luck.

Its theories of perception, attribution, cognition, and decision making explain why individuals often experience similar uncertain events in remarkably different ways.

For the **Research on Luck** project, psychological research demonstrates that objective uncertainty alone cannot explain human experiences of luck.

The following reviews therefore examine how psychological theories of perception, cognitive bias, attribution, agency, and decision making contribute to a broader interdisciplinary understanding of luck.

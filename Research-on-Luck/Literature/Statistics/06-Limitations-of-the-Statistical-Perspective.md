# Limitations of the Statistical Perspective

## Abstract

Statistics provides powerful mathematical methods for representing uncertainty, analyzing variation, and drawing inferences from incomplete information. These methods have transformed scientific research across nearly every discipline. At the same time, statistical analysis necessarily operates within methodological boundaries that limit what can be concluded from empirical evidence alone.

This review examines the principal limitations of the statistical perspective and considers their implications for the broader **Research on Luck** project. It argues that statistical methods describe uncertainty and evaluate evidence but cannot, by themselves, establish conceptual definitions, identify complete causal mechanisms, or determine the broader significance of uncertain events.

---

# 1. Introduction

Modern statistics has become one of the foundational sciences of empirical investigation.

Through probability theory, inference, estimation, and predictive modeling, statistics enables researchers to reason rigorously under uncertainty.

Its influence extends across the natural sciences, engineering, medicine, economics, psychology, and the social sciences.

Nevertheless, statistical reasoning addresses only certain aspects of scientific inquiry.

Recognizing these boundaries is essential for interpreting empirical evidence responsibly.

---

# 2. Dependence on Assumptions

Every statistical analysis depends upon assumptions.

These may include assumptions concerning:

* probability distributions,
* sampling,
* independence,
* model structure,
* measurement,
* causal relationships.

When these assumptions are violated, statistical conclusions may become unreliable.

Consequently, statistical results should always be interpreted within the context of their underlying assumptions.

---

# 3. Models Are Simplifications

Statistical models are representations of reality rather than reality itself.

No model captures every relevant variable or interaction.

Instead, models balance:

* simplicity,
* interpretability,
* predictive performance,
* computational feasibility.

This simplification is both a strength and an unavoidable limitation.

---

# 4. Evidence Does Not Establish Causation

Statistical evidence can reveal:

* associations,
* patterns,
* predictive relationships,
* uncertainty.

However, these findings do not automatically establish causal mechanisms.

Determining why events occur frequently requires:

* theory,
* experimental design,
* domain knowledge,
* interdisciplinary investigation.

Statistics therefore contributes evidence rather than complete explanation.

---

# 5. Quantification Has Limits

Not every scientifically meaningful concept can be represented directly by numerical measures.

Concepts such as:

* justice,
* responsibility,
* meaning,
* opportunity,
* institutional quality,
* luck,

often require conceptual interpretation in addition to statistical analysis.

Attempting to reduce these concepts entirely to measurable variables risks oversimplifying the underlying phenomena.

---

# 6. Human Interpretation

Statistical results do not interpret themselves.

Researchers must decide:

* which questions to investigate,
* which variables to measure,
* which models to construct,
* how conclusions should be interpreted.

Consequently, scientific inference inevitably involves human judgment alongside statistical methodology.

---

# 7. Contributions to the Research on Luck Project

Recognizing these limitations strengthens rather than weakens statistical reasoning.

For the **Research on Luck** project, statistics provides indispensable methods for representing uncertainty, evaluating evidence, and comparing competing explanations.

At the same time, conceptual questions concerning luck require contributions from philosophy, empirical questions require economics and related disciplines, behavioral questions require psychology, and system-level questions require complexity science.

Statistics therefore serves as one component of an interdisciplinary framework rather than a complete theory of luck.

---

# 8. Open Questions

Several important questions remain beyond the scope of statistics alone.

* Can uncertainty always be represented mathematically?
* How should statistical evidence be integrated with conceptual reasoning?
* Which aspects of luck are fundamentally non-statistical?
* How should qualitative evidence complement quantitative analysis?
* Can statistical models adequately represent adaptive and evolving social systems?

These questions motivate continued interdisciplinary research.

---

# 9. Conclusion

Statistics provides one of the most rigorous methodological foundations in modern science.

Its theories of probability, inference, and uncertainty have transformed empirical research and substantially improved our ability to analyze complex phenomena.

At the same time, statistical methods necessarily operate within conceptual, observational, and methodological boundaries.

For the **Research on Luck** project, recognizing these limitations reinforces an important principle: statistics is indispensable for studying uncertainty, but it cannot by itself define, explain, or fully characterize the phenomenon of luck.

A comprehensive understanding of luck therefore requires integrating statistical methodology with conceptual analysis, empirical investigation, behavioral science, and systems thinking.

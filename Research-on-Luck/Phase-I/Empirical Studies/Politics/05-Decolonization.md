# 05 – Decolonization: Self-Determination and the Reorganization of Political Order

## Overview

The twentieth century witnessed one of the largest political transformations in human history as colonial empires gradually gave way to independent nation-states across Asia, Africa, the Middle East, and the Caribbean. Decolonization fundamentally altered international politics, expanded the principle of national self-determination, and reshaped global institutions.

Traditional historical accounts often emphasize nationalist movements, colonial decline, or international diplomacy. From the perspective of the *Research on Luck* project, however, decolonization illustrates a broader political mechanism: the reorganization of political order. Political systems evolve not only through internal institutional reform but also through changes in the distribution of political authority among societies.

This case demonstrates that political adaptation may occur simultaneously at domestic and international levels, producing entirely new political environments for both newly independent states and the international system itself.

---

# Historical Background

For several centuries, European empires exercised political control over large portions of the world.

Following the Second World War, however, multiple developments accelerated decolonization.

These included:

* Economic exhaustion of colonial powers.
* Rising nationalist movements.
* International recognition of self-determination.
* Changing geopolitical competition.
* Expanding global institutions.
* Increasing political mobilization within colonial societies.

Over subsequent decades, dozens of new sovereign states emerged, fundamentally altering the structure of international politics.

---

# Political Self-Determination

Decolonization expanded the principle that political authority should derive from the people governed rather than external imperial control.

This transformation involved more than territorial independence.

New governments faced the challenge of establishing:

* Constitutional institutions.
* Administrative capacity.
* National identity.
* Legal systems.
* Economic governance.
* International diplomacy.
* Public legitimacy.

Political independence therefore represented the beginning rather than the completion of institutional development.

---

# Institution Building

Newly independent states inherited widely different historical conditions.

Some possessed:

* Established administrative institutions.
* Educational systems.
* Relatively cohesive political identities.

Others confronted:

* Weak governmental capacity.
* Artificial colonial borders.
* Ethnic and linguistic diversity.
* Limited infrastructure.
* Economic dependence.
* External geopolitical pressures.

Consequently, political development diverged significantly despite similar experiences of independence.

Institution building became one of the defining adaptive challenges of the post-colonial era.

---

# Reorganization of the International System

Decolonization transformed not only domestic politics but also international governance.

The emergence of numerous independent states influenced:

* International law.
* Global diplomacy.
* Development institutions.
* Trade relationships.
* Security arrangements.
* Multilateral organizations.

Political authority became increasingly distributed across a much larger number of sovereign actors.

The global political system itself evolved through this redistribution.

---

# Luck and Political Development

Decolonization was exposed to consequential uncertainty in multiple ways.

The timing of global wars, geopolitical competition, economic conditions, leadership transitions, and international diplomatic developments created opportunities for independence that were not equally available across all regions.

However, long-term political outcomes depended less upon the achievement of independence itself than upon the capacity of new states to establish effective institutions, maintain legitimacy, and adapt to changing domestic and international environments.

Global war, imperial capacity, mobilization, diplomacy, and geopolitical competition changed the feasibility and timing of independence. Post-independence trajectories emerged through inherited boundaries, institutions, conflict, external intervention, policy choices, resources, and further uncertainty.

---

# Architecture-Alignment Assessment

## Systems, Boundaries, and Political Agency

Colonial empires, colonial administrations, anticolonial movements, local communities, emergent states, and international institutions operated at different levels. Decolonization should not portray independence as an opportunity passively supplied by external events: organized movements created political pressure under coercive conditions. Colonial boundaries and administrative structures also shaped the systems later evaluated as states.

## Opportunity, Constraint, and Ex Ante Uncertainty

Independence trajectories involved potential, access, recognition, mobilization, negotiation or conflict, realization, and possible foreclosure. Actors faced uncertainty about imperial repression, coalition cohesion, borders, administrative succession, economic viability, foreign intervention, and international recognition. Formal sovereignty expanded some opportunity sets while inherited dependency, conflict, debt, and geopolitical competition constrained others.

## Selection, Counterfactuals, and Evaluation

The survival or economic performance of postcolonial states cannot by itself identify institutional capability or the quality of independence. Comparisons must account for colonial extraction, settler structures, war, border design, resource dependence, Cold War intervention, and selected observation of surviving movements and governments. Feasible alternatives include federal arrangements, different borders, negotiated transitions, delayed independence, and other international guarantees. “Success” requires explicit, plural criteria and distributional analysis rather than retrospective ranking by stability or growth alone.

---

# Lessons

Several broader principles emerge.

First, political sovereignty alone does not ensure effective governance.

Second, institution building is a long-term adaptive process extending well beyond constitutional independence.

Third, historical legacies continue to influence political development long after formal political transitions.

Fourth, international institutions play an increasingly important role in shaping domestic political opportunities.

Finally, successful political development depends upon balancing national self-determination with participation in an increasingly interconnected international system.

---

# Connection to the Research on Luck

Decolonization reinforces one of the central propositions of the *Research on Luck* project:

> Luck may be attributed ex post when consequential uncertainty altered access to independence or later trajectories. Postcolonial outcomes require analysis of inherited structures, power, institution building, international constraints, distribution, and contested evaluative criteria.

Unlike the United States Constitution, which illustrates adaptive constitutional design; the French Revolution, which demonstrates transformation through changing legitimacy; European Integration, which explores cooperative governance; and Democratic Transitions, which examine institutional adaptation through political participation, decolonization illustrates a fifth mechanism of political evolution: **the redistribution of political authority through self-determination and institution building**.

Together, these first five political cases demonstrate that political evolution occurs through multiple pathways:

* **Adaptive constitutional design**
* **Transformation through legitimacy**
* **Cooperative governance**
* **Democratic institutional adaptation**
* **Reorganization through self-determination**

Collectively, they suggest that political systems evolve not only by reforming existing institutions but also by redefining who possesses political authority, how legitimacy is established, and how governance is organized within an evolving international order.

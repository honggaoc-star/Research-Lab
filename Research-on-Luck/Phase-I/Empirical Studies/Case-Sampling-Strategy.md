# Case Sampling Strategy

## Building a Comparative Evidence Corpus for Research on Luck

---

# Purpose

The Case Studies section provides the empirical evidence used to evaluate, refine, and challenge the theoretical development of the Research on Luck project.

Case studies are not the primary research product.

They are the evidence through which candidate mechanisms are investigated.

Accordingly, case selection is a methodological decision rather than a historical survey.

The objective is not to document every important discovery, technology, business, institution, or historical event.

The objective is to construct a sufficiently diverse comparative evidence corpus capable of supporting or refuting emerging theoretical explanations.

---

# Research Philosophy

The Research on Luck project develops theory through iterative comparison between conceptual work and empirical evidence.

Case studies therefore serve four complementary purposes:

* discover candidate mechanisms;
* test existing mechanisms;
* identify boundary conditions;
* challenge theoretical assumptions.

Cases are not included to illustrate preconceived conclusions.

Every case should have the potential to strengthen, modify, or contradict the evolving framework.

---

# Guiding Principle

Case selection follows one fundamental principle.

> **Maximize explanatory diversity rather than familiarity, historical prominence, or representational balance.**

The purpose of diversity is scientific.

Mechanisms that recur across substantially different environments provide stronger evidence than mechanisms observed repeatedly within similar contexts.

The goal is therefore to maximize variation that is theoretically informative.

---

# What Is Being Sampled?

The project does **not** sample history.

It samples evidence relevant to proposed mechanisms.

Consequently, the unit of analysis is neither the case itself nor its historical importance.

The unit of analysis is the explanatory contribution that the case makes to evaluating candidate mechanisms.

A famous case should not be selected simply because it is famous.

Likewise, a lesser-known case should not be excluded if it substantially strengthens the comparative corpus.

---

# Sampling Dimensions

Case selection should consider multiple dimensions simultaneously.

No single dimension determines selection.

## Geographic Diversity

Seek evidence from different world regions rather than concentrating on one country or civilization.

---

## Cultural Diversity

Include different cultural traditions, knowledge systems, and historical experiences whenever possible.

---

## Institutional Diversity

Seek variation across political, economic, organizational, and governance structures.

---

## Historical Diversity

Avoid excessive concentration within one historical era.

Where appropriate, include ancient, premodern, industrial, and contemporary contexts.

---

## Organizational Diversity

When organizations are studied, include different ownership structures, governance models, and organizational forms.

---

## Outcome Diversity

The corpus should intentionally include:

* success;
* failure;
* unrealized opportunity;
* delayed recognition;
* partial success;
* suppressed development;
* strategic reversal.

Failures are treated as evidence rather than exceptions.

---

## Mechanism Diversity

Each new case should contribute one or more of the following:

* discovery of a new mechanism;
* validation of an existing mechanism;
* challenge to an existing mechanism;
* clarification of boundary conditions;
* comparison between competing explanations.

---

# Case Selection Criteria

Before selecting a new case, the following questions should be considered.

* Which mechanism does this case investigate?
* What new explanatory value does this case contribute?
* Which sampling gaps does it reduce?
* Is reliable evidence available?
* Does it strengthen comparison with the existing corpus?
* Does it increase confidence in—or appropriately challenge—the emerging theoretical framework?

Cases should be selected because they improve the evidence corpus rather than because they are individually important.

---

# Corpus Development

The evidence corpus is expected to evolve continuously.

Early case selection naturally emphasized discovering candidate mechanisms.

As the repository matures, increasing emphasis will be placed upon validating those mechanisms across substantially different environments.

Consequently, future additions should prioritize underrepresented dimensions rather than simply increasing the number of examples.

---

# Corpus Size

The objective of the Case Studies section is not comprehensive historical coverage.

Instead, each domain seeks to develop a sufficiently diverse evidence corpus capable of evaluating the emerging theoretical framework.

As a general guideline, each domain should normally contain approximately **10–12 carefully selected cases**.

This range is not a fixed requirement.

Additional cases should be included only when they provide substantial new explanatory value that cannot reasonably be obtained from the existing corpus.

The emphasis is therefore placed on evidence quality and diversity rather than corpus size.

---

# Theoretical Saturation

Case collection should continue only while additional cases materially improve theoretical understanding.

A domain may be considered provisionally saturated when additional cases consistently reinforce existing mechanisms without substantially extending, refining, or challenging the current framework.

At that point, research effort should shift toward comparative synthesis, theoretical refinement, and identification of remaining evidence gaps.

Future discoveries may justify expanding the corpus again.

---

# Survivorship Bias

Successful and well-known cases are more likely to be documented and remembered.

This creates a significant risk of survivorship bias.

Each domain should therefore intentionally seek evidence involving:

* failure;
* unrealized opportunity;
* delayed recognition;
* abandoned strategies;
* unsuccessful innovation;
* suppressed development.

Negative evidence is considered equally valuable for theory development.

---

# Familiarity Bias

Researchers naturally select cases they already know.

This tendency may unintentionally overrepresent:

* Western societies;
* English-language literature;
* recent history;
* highly visible organizations;
* successful innovations.

Case selection should therefore deliberately explore underrepresented regions, traditions, institutions, and historical periods whenever reliable evidence is available.

---

# Comparative Synthesis

The purpose of Comparative Synthesis is not to summarize individual cases.

Its purpose is to evaluate the evidence corpus as a whole.

Specifically, Comparative Synthesis should identify:

* mechanisms consistently supported;
* mechanisms supported only under particular conditions;
* mechanisms requiring additional evidence;
* mechanisms no longer supported by the available corpus.

Every theoretical conclusion should remain proportional to the diversity and quality of its supporting evidence.

---

# Relationship to General Theory

General Theory represents the current interpretation of the available evidence.

It should never extend beyond what the evidence corpus can reasonably support.

Accordingly,

mechanisms repeatedly observed across diverse contexts receive increasing confidence.

Mechanisms supported primarily within narrow cultural, institutional, or historical settings should be regarded as provisional until broader comparative validation becomes available.

---

# Living Strategy

This sampling strategy is intended to evolve alongside the Research on Luck project.

As new domains are completed and additional evidence becomes available, sampling priorities may change.

Future revisions should strengthen methodological transparency while improving the ability of the evidence corpus to distinguish:

* universal mechanisms,
* context-dependent mechanisms,
* unsupported hypotheses,
* and new directions for investigation.

---

# Guiding Statement

The purpose of the Case Studies section is not to collect famous examples.

Its purpose is to construct a carefully designed comparative evidence corpus capable of evaluating the mechanisms proposed throughout the Research on Luck project.

Every case should earn its place by improving the explanatory power of the corpus.

The strength of the General Theory will ultimately depend not upon the number of cases collected, but upon the diversity, quality, and comparative value of the evidence they collectively provide.

# System and Environment

## Purpose

This document develops the relationship between **System** and **Environment**, two of the four working primitives established in [10-Working-Primitives.md](10-Working-Primitives.md).

The distinction is necessary for analysis, but it is not assumed to be absolute or permanent. A system is identified relative to a research question, and its environment consists of relevant conditions and entities outside the chosen boundary. Repeated interaction may change both sides of that boundary.

---

## 1. System

A **system** is an analytically bounded configuration of components, relations, capacities, and states that persists or changes through time.

A system may be:

- an individual;
- a team or organization;
- an institution;
- a scientific community;
- a technological ecosystem;
- a political order;
- an economy;
- or another configuration appropriate to the question.

Calling something a system does not imply perfect integration, fixed purpose, equilibrium, or internal harmony. Systems may contain conflict, hierarchy, loosely connected components, and changing boundaries.

Every study should specify:

1. the focal system;
2. its boundary;
3. its level of analysis;
4. its relevant internal components;
5. its state at the beginning of the analysis;
6. and the time horizon over which persistence or change is examined.

---

## 2. Environment

The **environment** is the set of conditions, entities, structures, and processes outside the focal system’s analytical boundary that affect—or could affect—its states, interactions, opportunities, constraints, or trajectory.

Depending on the case, the environment may include:

- physical and geographic conditions;
- other systems;
- institutions and laws;
- social networks;
- scientific knowledge;
- technological infrastructures;
- markets;
- political arrangements;
- cultural expectations;
- resource distributions;
- and historical legacies.

The environment is not a residual category containing everything that the model leaves unexplained. Only conditions relevant to the focal relationship and time horizon should be included.

---

## 3. Analytical Boundaries

System–environment boundaries are selected, not merely discovered.

A firm may be the focal system when studying market adaptation. The same firm may be part of the environment when studying an employee. A government may be an environmental institution in one case and the focal adaptive system in another.

Boundary choices affect:

- what counts as internal capability;
- what counts as external exposure;
- where agency is located;
- which feedback is visible;
- and how luck is attributed.

For this reason, boundary selection should be explicit and open to revision.

A useful boundary should:

- correspond to the research question;
- preserve causally important internal relations;
- avoid hiding relevant heterogeneity;
- and permit meaningful comparison across cases.

---

## 4. System State and Trajectory

A **state** describes the system at a selected time. It may include structure, resources, information, capabilities, relationships, rules, or position.

A **trajectory** is a sequence of states and transitions.

Luck is often more intelligible at the trajectory level than at the level of a single outcome. A small interaction may change later opportunity access and produce large cumulative effects. A large shock may have limited durable effect if the system absorbs it.

Analyses should therefore distinguish:

- initial state;
- immediate change;
- intermediate transitions;
- feedback;
- and longer-term trajectory.

The evaluative meaning of an event may change across horizons.

---

## 5. Open and Adaptive Systems

The systems studied in this project are generally open: they exchange information, resources, influence, or risk with their environments.

They may also be adaptive. An adaptive system can alter behavior, structure, or relationships in response to actual or anticipated conditions.

Openness and adaptation are not guarantees of success. A system may:

- misperceive its environment;
- adapt to obsolete conditions;
- become locked into a path;
- lack resources to respond;
- or change the environment in ways that create new vulnerabilities.

Adaptive capacity is developed separately in [14-Core-Assumptions.md](14-Core-Assumptions.md) and situated within the overall architecture in [15-Conceptual-Summary.md](15-Conceptual-Summary.md).

---

## 6. Interaction and Mutual Change

The system–environment distinction does not imply one-way causation.

Through interaction, environments affect systems and systems affect environments:

```text
System state
    ↓
Interaction with environment
    ↓
Changed system and/or environment
    ↓
New interaction conditions
    ↓
Further interaction
```

Systems may create technologies, institutions, markets, classifications, and information that alter the exposures of other systems. Institutions may redistribute opportunity and risk. Successful responses may generate feedback that changes future selection conditions.

The environment is therefore partly endogenous in many cases.

---

## 7. Nested Systems

Systems are frequently nested.

An individual may belong to an organization, which operates within an institution, market, polity, or scientific field. Variation at one level may become environment at another.

Nested analysis should distinguish:

- within-system variation;
- between-system interaction;
- cross-level influence;
- and aggregation.

An outcome favorable to one component may be adverse to the larger system. Likewise, system-level resilience may conceal unequal burdens among components.

Moving between levels without explanation risks attributing institutional effects to individuals or individual actions to entire systems.

---

## 8. Opportunity and Constraint as Relations

An opportunity is not simply an environmental object. It is a relation between:

- a system with particular capabilities and objectives;
- an environment containing particular conditions;
- and a possible interaction that could alter accessible trajectories.

A constraint is likewise relational. The same rule, technology, or resource distribution may constrain one system, enable another, and remain irrelevant to a third.

This relationship is developed in [13-Opportunity-and-Constraints.md](13-Opportunity-and-Constraints.md).

---

## 9. Institutions, Power, and Resources

Institutions, power, and resources are essential but are not currently treated as separate primitives.

Provisionally:

- institutions may be system structures, environmental structures, or focal systems;
- power describes asymmetries in the capacity to shape interactions, boundaries, rules, and environments;
- resources are system properties or environmental conditions that affect feasible interaction;
- information may be held by a system, distributed through an environment, or transmitted through interaction.

This classification remains open to revision. If repeated analysis shows that any of these cannot be represented without distortion, the primitive set should be reconsidered.

---

## 10. Implications for Luck

Within this architecture, luck is system-relative.

The same environmental change may generate:

- favorable exposure for one system;
- adverse exposure for another;
- an unrealized opportunity for a third;
- and no consequential change for a fourth.

Analysis should not ask only, “What happened?” It should ask:

1. Which system was affected?
2. What environment was relevant?
3. How was the boundary drawn?
4. Through what interaction did change occur?
5. What was uncertain?
6. Which state or accessible trajectory changed?
7. How did later feedback alter the consequence?

---

## 11. Research Guidance

Empirical cases should report:

- focal system and level;
- boundary justification;
- initial system state;
- relevant environmental conditions;
- interaction channels;
- system response or non-response;
- environmental feedback;
- and alternative plausible trajectories.

[Formalization](../Formalization/README.md) should represent boundary, state, interaction, and transition assumptions explicitly. [Measurement](../Measurement/README.md) should avoid using indicators from different levels as if they measured the same construct.

---

## Conclusion

System and Environment are distinct for analytical purposes but dynamically interdependent.

Their boundary must be chosen explicitly, their levels must be kept clear, and their mutual change must be represented through interaction. This provides the foundation for analyzing uncertainty, opportunity, capability, adaptation, and luck without treating environmental events or system outcomes as self-explanatory.

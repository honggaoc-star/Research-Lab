# Working Primitives

## Purpose

This document establishes a minimal set of working primitives for the **Research on Luck** project.

The project has developed a large conceptual vocabulary: luck, uncertainty, contingency, opportunity, exposure, recognition, adaptation, selection, path dependence, resilience, and transformation. These concepts are useful, but they do not all occupy the same analytical level.

Without a simpler underlying architecture, foundational terms can overlap, causal claims can become difficult to compare, and empirical findings can accumulate without a clear route into general theory.

The working primitives provide that underlying architecture.

They are:

1. **System**
2. **Environment**
3. **Interaction**
4. **Uncertainty**

These primitives are not presented as final metaphysical categories or as a complete theory of luck. They are provisional analytical starting points chosen because they appear broad enough to organize the project and simple enough to remain operational.

Their value must ultimately be judged by whether they improve explanation, formalization, measurement, and comparative empirical analysis.

---

## Why Working Primitives Are Needed

The early Foundations documents began with luck itself: what luck means, how it differs from randomness, and how it relates to risk, uncertainty, and control.

The empirical and comparative studies later shifted attention toward a more dynamic problem:

> How do systems operating under uncertainty encounter, interpret, and respond to changes in their environments, and how do those interactions alter their possible trajectories?

This shift revealed that luck cannot be analyzed adequately as an isolated event or outcome.

A discovery, crisis, encounter, policy change, technological opening, or market transition becomes consequential only in relation to:

- a system that is affected;
- an environment in which possibilities and constraints arise;
- interactions through which exposure and response occur;
- and uncertainty concerning conditions, processes, or consequences.

The four primitives therefore sit beneath the project’s more specific concepts. They provide a common grammar for asking what exists in the analysis, what changes, what relates to what, and what remains unresolved or indeterminate.

---

## Status of the Primitives

The term **working** is essential.

The primitives are:

- conceptually prior within the present framework;
- analytically useful across multiple domains;
- open to refinement or replacement;
- and subject to empirical and theoretical testing.

A primitive should not be multiplied merely because an important concept exists.

Institutions, agents, resources, information, opportunities, capabilities, events, and outcomes may all be indispensable to particular analyses. The initial question, however, is whether they can be derived as types, properties, states, or relations involving the four working primitives.

A new primitive should be introduced only when repeated analysis shows that an essential phenomenon cannot be represented without distortion under the existing architecture.

## 1. System

### Definition

A **system** is an analytically bounded configuration of components, relations, capacities, and states that persists or changes through time.

The system is the focal entity whose exposure, response, or trajectory is being studied.

Depending on the research question, a system may be:

- an individual;
- a research team;
- an organization;
- a firm;
- an institution;
- a scientific community;
- a technological ecosystem;
- a political order;
- an economy;
- or a civilization.

The boundary is analytical rather than automatically given. The same entity may be treated as a system in one investigation and as part of the environment in another.

### Principal Features

A system may possess:

- structure;
- internal components;
- resources;
- information;
- rules;
- capabilities;
- memory;
- objectives;
- adaptive capacity;
- and a current state.

These are not necessarily separate primitives. They describe how a particular system is constituted and what it can do.

### Boundary and Level of Analysis

Every study should state:

1. what the focal system is;
2. where its analytical boundary is drawn;
3. the level at which it is being studied;
4. the time horizon of interest;
5. and which internal differences are relevant.

This prevents movement between individual, organizational, institutional, and systemic explanations without acknowledgment.

### System State and Trajectory

A system has a state at a given time, but luck is rarely understood adequately from a single state or outcome.

The more important object is often the **trajectory**: the sequence of states and transitions through which the system develops.

A contingent event may have a small immediate effect but a large long-term consequence if it changes later options, feedback processes, or exposure. Conversely, a dramatic event may leave little durable effect when the system absorbs or reverses it.

## 2. Environment

### Definition

The **environment** is the set of conditions, entities, structures, and processes outside the focal system’s analytical boundary that affect—or could affect—its states, actions, opportunities, constraints, or trajectory.

The environment is not merely a passive background. It may change independently, respond to the system, or be partly transformed by the system’s actions.

### Environmental Contents

Depending on the domain, the environment may include:

- physical conditions;
- other individuals or organizations;
- institutions and laws;
- scientific knowledge;
- technological infrastructures;
- markets;
- political arrangements;
- cultural expectations;
- resource distributions;
- historical legacies;
- and ongoing stochastic processes.

### Opportunity and Constraint

Opportunities and constraints are relational properties of a system in an environment.

An environmental condition is not an opportunity in itself. It becomes an opportunity when a system could, under relevant conditions, use or respond to it in a way that changes its possible trajectory.

Likewise, a condition becomes a constraint relative to particular system states, capabilities, objectives, or possible actions.

This relational view helps explain why the same environmental development may benefit one system, harm another, and remain irrelevant to a third.

### Endogenous Environmental Change

The distinction between system and environment does not imply one-way causation.

Systems may:

- modify their environments;
- construct institutions;
- create technologies;
- change markets;
- reshape information flows;
- alter the exposure of other systems;
- and generate new uncertainty.

The environment must therefore be treated as potentially endogenous to repeated system–environment interaction.

---

## 3. Interaction

### Definition

An **interaction** is a relation or process through which a system and its environment—or multiple systems—affect one another.

Interaction is the bridge between existence and consequence.

Environmental variation cannot influence a system without some channel of exposure, transmission, perception, interpretation, or material effect. Similarly, a system cannot adapt to or alter its environment except through interaction.

### Forms of Interaction

Interactions may include:

- exposure;
- encounter;
- observation;
- communication;
- exchange;
- competition;
- cooperation;
- conflict;
- intervention;
- experimentation;
- selection;
- learning;
- and feedback.

An interaction may be deliberate or unintended, direct or mediated, brief or repeated, symmetrical or highly unequal.

### Interaction and Mechanism

A mechanism should normally specify how an interaction changes a system, its environment, or their relationship.

For example:

- an encounter transmits information;
- a crisis removes an existing constraint;
- a policy redistributes exposure;
- an experiment reveals a previously unknown effect;
- a market response amplifies an early advantage;
- or an institution suppresses the consequences of adverse variation.

This makes interaction central to causal explanation rather than a residual link between an event and an outcome.

### Recursive Interaction

Many important processes are recursive:

```text
System state
    ↓
Interaction with environment
    ↓
Changed system and/or environment
    ↓
New interaction conditions
    ↓
Further change
```

Through repetition, initially small differences may be amplified, stabilized, reversed, or rendered irrelevant.

Path dependence, cumulative advantage, institutional lock-in, resilience, and transformation are therefore not primitive facts. They are possible long-term patterns produced through sequences of interaction.

---

## 4. Uncertainty

### Definition

**Uncertainty** is a condition in which relevant states, processes, events, relationships, or consequences are not fully determined, known, predictable, or controllable from the perspective and time horizon of the analysis.

Uncertainty creates a space in which more than one future trajectory remains possible or cannot be ruled out.

### Forms of Uncertainty

Uncertainty may concern:

- which event will occur;
- when it will occur;
- which systems will be exposed;
- how an event will be interpreted;
- what actions will be taken;
- how interactions will unfold;
- whether feedback will amplify or suppress an effect;
- and which long-term consequences will result.

It may arise from stochastic processes, incomplete information, complexity, strategic behavior, novelty, changing institutions, measurement limits, or genuine indeterminacy.

### Uncertainty and Risk

Risk is a structured form of uncertainty in which possible outcomes and their probabilities can be estimated or modeled with some defensibility.

The present framework is broader. Luck may arise under measurable risk, but it may also arise under ambiguity, novelty, model uncertainty, or conditions in which the relevant possibilities were not specified in advance.

### Uncertainty and Perspective

Uncertainty may differ across systems and observers.

An event may be predictable to one actor but unforeseen by another. A process may be determined at one analytical level yet function as uncertainty at another because the affected system lacks relevant knowledge or control.

Claims about uncertainty should therefore identify:

- uncertainty for whom or what;
- at what time;
- with what available information;
- at which level of analysis;
- and over what horizon.

## Relationships Among the Primitives

The primitives acquire explanatory value through their relationships.

A minimal representation is:

```text
System ↔ Interaction ↔ Environment
              │
        under Uncertainty
```

This representation should not be interpreted as a fixed linear sequence.

The system and environment are analytically distinguished but dynamically connected. Interaction changes one or both. Uncertainty affects what interactions occur, how they are understood, and what consequences follow.

The four primitives support several basic classes of relationship:

### Exposure Relationships

How and why does a system come into contact with an environmental condition, event, or process?

Exposure may be distributed unequally across systems because of location, institutional position, network structure, inherited conditions, or previous choices.

### Interpretive Relationships

How does a system recognize, classify, or misunderstand what it encounters?

The consequences of variation often depend on whether a development is noticed and whether its significance is understood.

### Response Relationships

What does the system do following exposure or recognition?

Responses may include action, delay, avoidance, experimentation, adaptation, institutionalization, or failure to respond.

### Feedback Relationships

How do initial effects change later conditions?

Feedback may amplify advantage, compound harm, stabilize a new practice, attract resources, alter expectations, or generate countervailing forces.

### Selection Relationships

Which systems, practices, technologies, or institutional forms persist under changing conditions, and why?

Observed survival does not by itself establish superiority. Selection may reflect contingent exposure, timing, inherited position, power, or environmental fit.

### Transformation Relationships

When do repeated interactions change the identity, structure, capabilities, or environment of a system sufficiently to produce a new regime or trajectory?

## Deriving the Project’s Major Concepts

The working primitives allow more specific concepts to be defined without treating each as foundationally independent.

### Event

An event is a temporally located change in a system, an environment, or their relationship.

### Exposure

Exposure is an interaction through which an environmental condition or event can affect a system.

### Opportunity

An opportunity is a system–environment relationship in which a possible interaction could expand or improve a system’s accessible trajectories relative to a specified objective or evaluative standard.

### Constraint

A constraint is a system–environment relationship that restricts accessible actions or trajectories.

### Capability

A capability is a property of a system that affects what interactions it can undertake and how it can recognize, respond to, or shape environmental conditions.

### Adaptation

Adaptation is a change in a system, its behavior, or its relationships that occurs in response to actual or anticipated environmental conditions.

### Selection

Selection is a process through which interactions under environmental conditions produce differential persistence, replication, growth, or disappearance.

### Path Dependence

Path dependence is a trajectory pattern in which earlier states or interactions alter the accessibility or consequences of later alternatives.

### Outcome

An outcome is an evaluated state or change observed at a specified time. It should not be confused with the full process that produced it.

### Luck

Within this architecture:

> **Luck is consequential variation in a system’s state, relationships, accessible opportunities, constraints, or trajectory arising through interactions under uncertainty and not fully determined by the system’s prior intentions, capabilities, or actions.**

This formulation preserves the project’s existing working conception while locating its elements within a more general architecture.

## A Dynamic Sequence for Analysis

Many cases can be investigated using the following sequence:

```text
Prior system state and environment
                ↓
       Uncertainty and variation
                ↓
      Exposure through interaction
                ↓
 Recognition and interpretation
                ↓
     Response or non-response
                ↓
 Feedback, adaptation, and selection
                ↓
     Changed trajectory or regime
                ↓
 Retrospective evaluation as good luck,
 bad luck, missed opportunity, or discovery
```

This is an analytical template, not a claim that every case follows a single causal order.

Interactions may be simultaneous, feedback may occur at multiple stages, and systems may actively generate the conditions later interpreted as luck.

---

## Ex Ante and Ex Post Analysis

Luck is frequently assigned retrospectively.

After an outcome is known, observers may construct a coherent narrative in which success appears inevitable, capability appears decisive, or failure appears predictable. This can obscure uncertainty that existed beforehand and alternatives that remained possible.

Analysis should therefore distinguish:

### Ex Ante Position

- What did the system know?
- What could it control?
- Which alternatives were accessible?
- Which uncertainties were recognized?
- Which outcomes were considered possible?

### Ex Post Position

- What occurred?
- Which interactions proved consequential?
- What feedback followed?
- Which alternatives disappeared?
- How was the outcome later interpreted?

The difference between these perspectives is essential for studying survivorship bias, unrealized opportunities, missed discoveries, and counterfactual trajectories.

## Implications for Formalization

Formalization should focus on explicit relationships among the primitives rather than attempting to formalize luck as an isolated residual.

Models may specify:

- system states and boundaries;
- environmental states and changes;
- interaction rules;
- information and control limits;
- uncertainty structures;
- transition probabilities or non-probabilistic alternatives;
- feedback mechanisms;
- and trajectory-dependent effects.

Different formal approaches may be appropriate for different questions, including causal models, dynamic systems, stochastic processes, agent-based models, networks, evolutionary models, and counterfactual analysis.

No single formal language is assumed to capture every relevant relationship.

---

## Implications for Measurement

Measurement should operationalize the components of the architecture separately where possible.

Empirical work should distinguish:

- prior system state;
- environmental conditions;
- exposure;
- uncertainty;
- recognition;
- response;
- feedback;
- intermediate change;
- and long-term outcome.

Luck should not be measured simply as the unexplained remainder after known predictors are included.

Residual variation may contain measurement error, omitted variables, misspecified relationships, or processes occurring at an unobserved level. A defensible empirical claim about luck requires evidence concerning uncertainty, limited prior control, consequential interaction, and altered trajectory.

---

## Implications for Empirical and Comparative Studies

Each empirical case should identify:

1. the focal system;
2. the relevant environment;
3. the system boundary and level of analysis;
4. the principal uncertainty;
5. the consequential interactions;
6. the opportunities or constraints that changed;
7. the system’s recognition and response;
8. the feedback or selection processes;
9. the resulting trajectory;
10. and plausible unrealized alternatives.

Comparative Studies should then ask which relationships recur across Science, Technology, Business, History, and Politics.

Recurring relationships may become candidates for General Theory. Domain-specific mechanisms should remain domain-specific unless comparison supports broader application.

---

## Criteria for Revising the Primitive Set

The four-primitives architecture should be revised if sustained analysis shows that it:

- cannot represent an essential phenomenon without conceptual distortion;
- repeatedly collapses distinct causal roles;
- prevents meaningful operationalization;
- creates systematic ambiguity across levels of analysis;
- or fails to improve comparison across domains.

Possible additions should not be promoted because they are important alone. They should be considered only when they are irreducible for the purposes of the research program.

Institutions, power, agency, information, resources, time, and population structure are especially important candidates for continued examination. At present, they can provisionally be represented as system properties, environmental structures, interaction conditions, or dimensions of uncertainty. That treatment remains a hypothesis to be tested, not a settled conclusion.

---

## Methodological Role

The working primitives establish a disciplined progression for the repository:

1. **Define primitives.**
2. **Derive domain-relevant concepts.**
3. **Specify relationships and mechanisms.**
4. **Formalize testable propositions.**
5. **Operationalize the components.**
6. **Test them in empirical cases.**
7. **Compare results across domains.**
8. **Promote only supported relationships into General Theory.**

This progression does not make research strictly linear.

Empirical evidence may force revisions to definitions. Measurement problems may expose theoretical ambiguity. Comparative analysis may reveal missing relationships or inappropriate levels of abstraction.

The architecture is therefore recursive and self-correcting.

---

## Current Working Proposition

The project’s current architectural proposition is:

> Systems interact with changing environments under uncertainty. These interactions alter exposure, information, opportunities, constraints, capabilities, and feedback conditions. Through repeated adaptation and selection, contingent differences may be amplified, suppressed, reversed, or stabilized into divergent trajectories. Luck is the evaluative and explanatory language applied to consequential variation within these processes when the variation was not fully determined by the affected system’s prior intentions, capabilities, or actions.

This proposition is deliberately broad.

Its purpose is to organize investigation, not to predetermine empirical conclusions.

The next task is to determine which relationships within this architecture are recurrent, measurable, and sufficiently supported to contribute to a general theory of luck in adaptive systems.

# Uncertainty

## Purpose

This document develops **Uncertainty** as a working primitive in the new conceptual architecture.

Uncertainty is not identical to randomness, risk, ignorance, surprise, or luck. It is the broader condition within which systems interact with changing environments while relevant states, processes, alternatives, or consequences remain incompletely determined, known, predictable, or controllable.

See [10-Working-Primitives.md](10-Working-Primitives.md) for the primitive set and [11-System-and-Environment.md](11-System-and-Environment.md) for the focal relationship within which uncertainty is assessed.

---

## 1. Working Definition

> **Uncertainty is a condition in which relevant states, processes, events, relationships, alternatives, or consequences are not fully determined, known, predictable, or controllable from the perspective and time horizon of the analysis.**

This definition deliberately combines several sources of incompleteness. Later formalization and measurement should separate them when possible.

Uncertainty should always be indexed:

- uncertainty for which system or observer;
- at what time;
- given what information;
- at which level of analysis;
- over what horizon;
- and concerning which relationship or consequence.

---

## 2. Forms of Uncertainty

### Stochastic uncertainty

Variation is represented probabilistically or treated as generated by a stochastic process.

### Epistemic uncertainty

Relevant facts, states, or mechanisms are unknown.

### Model uncertainty

Competing models imply different structures, predictions, or interpretations.

### Ambiguity

Possible outcomes are partly recognized, but probabilities or consequences are unclear.

### Novelty

Relevant possibilities are absent from the system’s prior categories or option set.

### Strategic uncertainty

Consequences depend on choices by other adaptive systems.

### Dynamic uncertainty

Feedback, learning, adaptation, and endogenous environmental change alter later possibilities.

### Possible indeterminacy

The framework leaves open whether some variation is irreducible rather than merely unknown.

These forms may coexist. Classifying one does not eliminate the others.

---

## 3. Risk and Uncertainty

Risk is a structured subset or representation of uncertainty.

A situation may be treated as risk when:

- relevant outcomes are specified;
- probabilities can be estimated with some defensibility;
- relationships are sufficiently stable;
- and a decision framework can compare alternatives.

Risk estimates remain conditional on data, models, classifications, and assumptions.

Uncertainty extends beyond risk when:

- possible outcomes are incomplete;
- probabilities are unstable or contested;
- environments are changing;
- other systems adapt strategically;
- or consequences emerge through feedback not represented ex ante.

The original distinction in [04-Luck-Risk-and-Uncertainty.md](04-Luck-Risk-and-Uncertainty.md) remains part of the repository’s conceptual history; this document locates it within the newer architecture.

---

## 4. Randomness and Uncertainty

Randomness concerns how variation is generated or represented. Uncertainty concerns limits of determination, knowledge, prediction, or control.

A random process creates uncertainty for some purposes, but uncertainty can also arise from:

- incomplete information;
- complexity;
- novelty;
- institutional opacity;
- strategic behavior;
- and changing system–environment relationships.

Conversely, a statistically random process may be well characterized at the population level even though individual realizations remain uncertain.

The distinction developed in [03-Luck-vs-Randomness.md](03-Luck-vs-Randomness.md) should therefore be preserved.

---

## 5. Ex Ante Uncertainty

Ex ante analysis reconstructs the position before a realized outcome is known.

It asks:

- What states and alternatives were recognized?
- What information was available?
- Which probabilities, if any, were defensible?
- What could the system influence or control?
- Which interactions were feasible?
- What constraints applied?
- Which consequences were anticipated?

This reconstruction is essential because ex post knowledge can make uncertain paths appear inevitable.

A system may act reasonably and receive an adverse outcome. It may act poorly and receive a favorable outcome. Evaluation of decisions should not be inferred solely from realization.

---

## 6. Transition Uncertainty

Uncertainty does not disappear when an initial event occurs.

During interaction, uncertainty may concern:

- who is exposed;
- what is perceived;
- how information is interpreted;
- which response is chosen;
- whether resources can be mobilized;
- how other systems react;
- and whether feedback amplifies or suppresses the initial effect.

This transition is where potential opportunities and constraints become realized, missed, transformed, or foreclosed.

---

## 7. Ex Post Knowledge and Attribution

After an outcome, observers know one realized trajectory. They do not automatically know:

- all feasible alternatives;
- the probability of the realized path;
- the contribution of capability;
- the contribution of exposure;
- or how small changes would have propagated.

Ex post attribution should separate:

1. what occurred;
2. what was uncertain beforehand;
3. what the system could influence;
4. which interactions became consequential;
5. and how later feedback changed the trajectory.

The project refers to this broader movement as:

```text
Ex ante: Luck Potential
        ↓
Transition: Luck Realization
        ↓
Ex post: Luck Attribution
```

Luck is not a primitive within this sequence. It is an evaluation and explanation of consequential variation arising through the sequence.

## 8. Uncertainty and Opportunity

An opportunity may exist relationally while its value, feasibility, or even existence remains uncertain.

Systems may face uncertainty about:

- whether an opportunity is real;
- whether they can access it;
- what capabilities it requires;
- how long it will remain open;
- how others will respond;
- and what later constraints it may create.

Search and experimentation can reduce some uncertainty while generating new information, interactions, and possibilities.

See [13-Opportunity-and-Constraints.md](13-Opportunity-and-Constraints.md).

---

## 9. Uncertainty and Adaptation

Adaptive systems may respond to uncertainty by:

- searching;
- learning;
- experimenting;
- diversifying;
- maintaining redundancy;
- delaying irreversible commitments;
- coordinating;
- or altering their environments.

These strategies do not eliminate uncertainty. They change exposure, information, response capacity, and the distribution of possible trajectories.

Adaptation can also fail or create new uncertainty. A response suited to prior conditions may become maladaptive after environmental change.

---

## 10. Levels of Uncertainty

Uncertainty at one level may coexist with regularity at another.

Examples include:

- unpredictable individual outcomes within stable population rates;
- uncertain firm survival within a patterned market transition;
- uncertain scientific discovery within a growing research field;
- or uncertain election outcomes within durable institutional structures.

Analyses should not infer individual predictability from aggregate regularity or system-level randomness from individual ignorance.

---

## 11. Measurement and Formalization

Formalization should state:

- where uncertainty enters;
- whether it is probabilistic, ambiguous, strategic, or model-based;
- which information is available to each system;
- whether transition rules are stable;
- and whether the environment changes endogenously.

Measurement should distinguish:

- uncertainty from measurement error;
- risk estimates from confidence in those estimates;
- unmodeled possibilities from rare modeled outcomes;
- and observer uncertainty from system uncertainty.

Luck should not be measured as unexplained residual variation without evidence connecting residual variation to uncertain interaction and consequential trajectory change.

---

## 12. Open Questions

The project should continue to investigate:

- When does uncertainty become sufficiently structured to be treated as risk?
- How should novelty be represented when possibilities are not specified ex ante?
- How do institutions redistribute uncertainty and exposure?
- Which adaptive capacities are robust across different uncertainty structures?
- When does reducing uncertainty improve outcomes, and when does it create rigidity?
- How can plausible unrealized trajectories be reconstructed without unconstrained speculation?
- At what point does a perspective-relative account of uncertainty become too weak for comparative analysis?

---

## Conclusion

Uncertainty is the enabling condition for divergent possible trajectories, but it is not itself luck.

Luck becomes relevant when interaction under uncertainty produces consequential variation for a system that was not fully determined by its prior intentions, capabilities, or actions. Preserving the ex ante, transition, and ex post distinction allows the project to study that process without allowing hindsight to erase uncertainty.
